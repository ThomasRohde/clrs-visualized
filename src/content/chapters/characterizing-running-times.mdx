Back in <a href={chapterHref('getting-started')}>chapter 2</a>, insertion sort's steps were counted exactly, and
the answer was a quadratic with six constants in it that depend on the machine. That is the last
time this book does that. Everything after it is written in the notation of this chapter, so it is
worth understanding precisely what that notation keeps and what it throws away.

It throws away **constant factors** and **lower-order terms**, and it says nothing whatever about
**small inputs**. What survives is the shape of the growth, which is the only part that is a
property of the algorithm rather than of the computer it ran on.

## §3.1 — Three symbols, informally

**O(g(n))** is an upper bound: f grows no faster than g. **Ω(g(n))** is a lower bound: no slower.
**Θ(g(n))** is both at once, and is the one you should reach for when you know the answer.

Two habits are worth forming immediately.

**Say which case you mean.** "Quicksort is O(n²)" and "quicksort is Θ(n lg n) on average" are both
true and are about different things. The notation describes a function; _which_ function — worst
case, average case, best case — is a separate choice you have to state. Insertion sort's worst case
is Θ(n²) and its best case is Θ(n), and both are exact.

**O is not "the answer".** Saying merge sort is O(n²) is true and useless, the way saying a journey
took under a week is true and useless. Θ is a claim; O alone is only half of one. The book uses O
where the lower bound is genuinely unknown or genuinely irrelevant, and Θ everywhere else — and
that distinction carries information.

## §3.2 — The definition, and the two things people misread

Formally, `f(n) = Θ(g(n))` means:

```
there exist positive constants c₁, c₂ and n₀
such that for all n ≥ n₀:   0 ≤ c₁·g(n) ≤ f(n) ≤ c₂·g(n)
```

Two quantifiers, in an order that matters. Both are visible in one picture.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="asymptotic-bound" size={24} />

What is plotted is the **ratio f(n)/n²**, not f itself. §3.2 draws f between the two curves c₁·n²
and c₂·n², and on paper that is the right picture; here it is not. Over the two dozen values of n it
takes to see f settle down, a quadratic grows by a factor of six hundred, so the crossing — the
entire point — happens in the bottom few pixels. Dividing through by n² is the same statement with
the scale removed: the band becomes two horizontal lines, the limit becomes a third, and n₀ is
visibly where the ratio enters the band for good.

**You choose the constants.** They are existentially quantified — you only have to exhibit _some_
c₁, c₂, n₀ that work, and they never have to be the tightest ones. The band here is c₁ = a/2 and
c₂ = 2a for the leading coefficient a, chosen with no cleverness whatever, precisely to show that no
cleverness is required.

**Nothing before n₀ counts.** Watch the ratio sit outside the band for the first few values of n. f
is still Θ(n²). An algorithm that spends a million steps on a fixed setup and then runs in linear
time is Θ(n), and that is not a loophole in the definition — it is what the definition is _for_,
because the setup cost stops being what you care about long before n gets large.

**And there is no such thing as _the_ n₀.** §3.2's own worked example takes f(n) = ½n² − 3n and
chooses c₁ = 1/14, c₂ = 1/2, arriving at n₀ = 7. Type `0.5, -3, 0` into the box and this player,
with its own blunter constants, gets **12** on the same function. Both are correct. n₀ is a
consequence of the constants you picked, not a property of f.

What makes the band inescapable is the shape of that ratio. It equals `a + b/n + c/n²`, so it
differs from the leading coefficient by at most `(|b| + |c|)/n` — which goes to zero however large b
and c are, and which is why the points flatten onto the dashed line at a. That inequality is checked
on every value of n the player tests, and it is the whole content of "lower-order terms do not
matter".

They do matter, enormously, for small n. **n₀ is exactly the measure of how long they go on
mattering**, and reading it as "the point where the asymptotics take over" is the most useful thing
you can do with it.

O and Ω are the same picture with one of the two lines rubbed out. Ω keeps the lower one, O the
upper — and the asymmetry in practice is that a wrong Ω claim is usually caught, while a loose O
claim usually goes unchallenged.

## §3.3 — What the shapes actually cost

The reason to care is that the differences are not subtle. Suppose one operation takes a microsecond
and you have a second to spend. The largest input each growth rate can finish:

```
   running time     largest n in one second
   lg n             astronomically large
   √n               10¹²
   n                1,000,000
   n lg n           62,746
   n²               1,000
   n³               100
   2ⁿ               19
   n!               9
```

Two readings, and the second is the one that changes decisions.

**Between Θ(n²) and Θ(n lg n) there is a factor of sixty in the size of problem you can handle.**
Between Θ(n lg n) and Θ(2ⁿ) there is no comparison at all — one of them scales and the other does
not, and a faster computer moves 19 to about 20.

**A better constant buys you a multiple; a better exponent buys you a category.** Doubling the
machine speed doubles the n an Θ(n) algorithm can handle, multiplies an Θ(n²) algorithm's n by 1.4,
and adds _one_ to an Θ(2ⁿ) algorithm's. This is why <a href={chapterHref('np-completeness')}>chapter 34</a> is a chapter about
categories rather than about speed.

A few standard facts the book leans on without re-deriving, all in §3.3:

- **logs**: base doesn't matter inside Θ, since log_a n = log_b n / log_b a is a constant factor —
  which is why the book writes lg n and stops worrying;
- **n! ≈ √(2πn)(n/e)ⁿ** (Stirling), so **lg(n!) = Θ(n lg n)** — the bound that makes
  <a href={chapterHref('sorting-in-linear-time')}>chapter 8</a>'s comparison-sorting lower bound
  come out at exactly the running time merge sort achieves;
- **any polynomial beats any polylogarithm, and any exponential beats any polynomial**: nᵇ = o(aⁿ)
  for a > 1, and lgᵇ n = o(nᵃ) for a > 0;
- **floors and ceilings** differ from the exact value by less than 1, which vanishes inside Θ — which
  is why the recurrences in chapter 4 can drop them.

<ComplexityCard id="asymptotic-bound">
  **Asymptotic notation is a statement about a limit, made useful for engineering.** It is precise,
  it is provable, and it deliberately answers a slightly different question from "how long will this
  take" — which is why measuring is still required, and why nobody should be surprised when an
  Θ(n lg n) sort loses to an Θ(n²) one on twenty elements.

**Θ is a claim; O is half of one.** Prefer Θ where you can prove it. When you see O in this book
where Θ would have fitted, the gap is usually deliberate and usually interesting.

**n₀ is not decoration.** For most algorithms it is small and can be ignored. For a few — matrix
multiplication by Strassen and its descendants, some FFT variants — it is large enough that the
asymptotically better algorithm is the wrong one to write, and the notation is telling you so if you
read it carefully.

</ComplexityCard>

## Where this shows up

Everywhere after this page. It is the vocabulary of every complexity card on this site, and the
reason a card can say "Θ(n lg n)" without asking which computer you have.

The interesting cases are the ones where the notation's own blind spots become the story. <a href={chapterHref('divide-and-conquer')}>Chapter 4</a>'s Strassen is asymptotically faster than
the obvious matrix multiplication and slower in practice below a few hundred rows — n₀ again. <a href={chapterHref('disjoint-sets')}>Chapter 19</a>'s union-find has an amortised bound involving
α(n), a function that is at most 4 for any input the universe can hold, so an asymptotic statement
about it is a statement about nothing you will ever run. And <a href={chapterHref('probabilistic-analysis')}>chapter 5</a>'s randomized analysis is what happens
when the worst case is real but so rare that describing an algorithm by it would be a lie of a
different kind.
