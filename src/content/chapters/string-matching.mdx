Find every place a pattern `P` of length m occurs in a text `T` of length n. Every editor, every
`grep`, every genome aligner does this, usually millions of times a second.

The first four algorithms here are unusually good company for each other, because they are four
different answers to the **same** criticism of the first one. They are also the clearest example in
the book of preprocessing: three of them spend time on the pattern before looking at the text at
all, and buy a linear scan with it. The fifth prepares the **text** instead, which is a different
trade entirely.

The first four are drawn the same way — the text on one row, the pattern on a second row shifted
right by the current offset — so they can be compared directly.

## §32.1 — The naive matcher

Try every shift. Compare until something differs.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="naive-string-matcher" size={13} />

Θ((n − m + 1)m) in the worst case, and the worst case is not exotic: `aaaa…a` against `aaab` hits it
exactly, because every shift matches almost all the way before failing on the last character.

**What is wasteful here is worth naming precisely**, because the next three sections are three ways
of not doing it. Having matched three characters at shift 3 and then failed, the naive matcher
starts again at shift 4 knowing **nothing** — even though what it just read tells it a great deal
about which shifts could possibly work.

It is worth saying that on random text over a large alphabet this is perfectly good: most shifts die
after one or two comparisons, and the expected cost is about 2(n − m + 1). It falls apart on
structured text with a small alphabet — DNA, log files, binary formats — which is exactly where
string matching is done at scale.

## §32.2 — Rabin-Karp

Compare **numbers** instead of strings.

Read the pattern as a number in base d, where d is the alphabet size; do the same for each window of
the text. Equal strings have equal numbers, so a window whose number differs is rejected in one
comparison rather than up to m.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="rabin-karp" size={13} />

Two problems, and their fixes are the algorithm.

**The numbers get too big**, so everything is done modulo a prime q, keeping each one inside a
machine word. **Modulo loses information**, so a hash hit is only a _candidate_ and must be
confirmed character by character. Those are **spurious hits**, and the run flags each one. With q
chosen well they are rare — about one shift in q — and the modulus here is deliberately small so you
will actually see some.

What makes it fast is the **rolling hash**: the window's number at shift s + 1 comes from the one at
shift s in constant time — drop the leading digit, shift, add the trailing one. All n − m + 1 window
numbers cost Θ(n) in total instead of Θ(nm).

The worst case is still Θ((n − m + 1)m), when every shift is spurious. That is why q is chosen at
random in practice: an adversary who knows q can construct that input, and one who does not, cannot.

## §32.3 — A finite automaton

Build a machine that reads the text once, one character at a time, and announces a match the moment
it reaches its final state. **Θ(n), one table lookup per character, and no character comparison at
all during the scan.**

<AlgorithmPlayer id="finite-automaton-matcher" size={12} />

The machine has m + 1 states, and state `q` means "the last q characters read are the pattern's
first q, and nothing longer works". The transition `δ(q, a)` is defined as the length of the longest
prefix of P that is a suffix of `P[1‥q]a` — which is exactly what keeps that invariant true.

Pause on the case that looks wrong. Reading one bad character can throw away most of the progress,
and **the state it drops to is not necessarily zero**: if the pattern's own prefix reappears inside
what has been read, the machine lands there instead. The table knows where, so the scan never looks
backwards.

The cost is the table: `m|Σ|` entries, and building it takes O(m|Σ|) with §32.3's method. On a small
alphabet that is nothing. On a large one it is the whole expense, and it is why the next section
exists.

This is also the shape of every lexer and every regular-expression engine you have used: compile the
pattern to an automaton once, then run the input through it in linear time.

## §32.4 — Knuth-Morris-Pratt

The automaton with the table replaced by **one array of m numbers**. Same Θ(n) scan, Θ(m) space, and
no dependence on the alphabet at all.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="kmp" size={13} />

`π[q]` is the length of the longest proper prefix of `P[1‥q]` that is also a suffix of it. When q
characters have matched and the next one fails, the algorithm does not go back to the next shift: it
knows the last `π[q]` characters it read are already a prefix of the pattern, and carries on from
there. **The text pointer never moves backwards**, which is what makes KMP the algorithm you want on
a stream — characters can be consumed once and discarded.

The first half of the run computes π, and it does it **by running the matcher on the pattern against
itself**. That is the neatest thing in the section and easy to miss when reading it: the two inner
loops are the same code with `T` replaced by `P`.

Watch the fallbacks in the second half. A single mismatch can fire `q = π[q]` several times in a
row, each giving up on a longer prefix, and every one of those is a whole block of shifts the naive
matcher would have tried individually. The bound is **amortised**: `q` rises by at most one per
character, so across the whole text it cannot fall more than n times, however dramatic any one
fallback looks.

## Comparing them

Run the naive matcher and KMP on the same input with the **trace tape** open — the strip under the
transport.

The naive tape is a regular sawtooth: a burst of comparisons, a mismatch, restart, repeat, with the
bursts all much the same length. KMP's is flatter and shorter, and the shape is different in a
specific way — there is no restart. Where the naive matcher's pointer jumps back into text it has
already read, KMP's simply carries on, and the tape has no corresponding beat because nothing is
re-examined.

Then compare the two **step counts**. On random text over three letters the gap is modest — the
naive matcher is not badly behaved here. Type a pathological input into both instead
(`aaaaaaaaaaaa, aaab`) and the difference is the whole chapter.

<ComplexityCard id="kmp">
  **Preprocessing buys a linear scan, and the four differ in what they pay for it**: nothing and
  Θ(nm) worst case; Θ(m) and an expected Θ(n); Θ(m|Σ|) and a guaranteed Θ(n); Θ(m) and a guaranteed
  Θ(n).

**KMP dominates the automaton** on space and preprocessing, at no cost in matching time. The
automaton is still the right mental model — KMP is best understood as the automaton with the
transitions computed on demand rather than stored.

**Rabin-Karp is the one that generalises.** The other three find one pattern; the rolling hash
extends naturally to finding any of a set of patterns, to two-dimensional matching, and to
content-addressed storage, where the "pattern" is a block of a file you have never seen.

</ComplexityCard>

## §32.5 — Suffix arrays

The last section inverts the problem, and it is the one that scales.

Everything above preprocesses the **pattern**. That is the right trade when the text is new each
time — a file you are grepping, a stream going past. It is the wrong trade when one large text is
searched over and over, which is what a search engine, a genome database and a code index all are.

A **suffix array** preprocesses the text instead: sort all n of its suffixes and keep their starting
positions. Every occurrence of any pattern is a prefix of some suffix, and the suffixes sharing a
given prefix are **contiguous in sorted order** — so a query becomes two binary searches for the
ends of that block, O(m lg n), with every occurrence found at once. Add the longest-common-prefix
array and it drops to O(m + lg n).

<AlgorithmPlayer id="suffix-array" size={9} />

The construction is the interesting part and the reason this is a section rather than a footnote.
Sorting n suffixes naively compares strings of total length Θ(n²), which would cost more than the
searching ever saves. **Prefix doubling** avoids it: sort by the first character, then use those
ranks to sort by the first two characters, then four, then eight — each round reusing the last one's
answer, so every comparison is two integers rather than a string.

Watch the highlighted window widen each round, and watch rows stop moving once their prefix is
unique. A suffix whose rank differs from its neighbour's is settled, and no later round can reorder
it — which is why ⌈lg n⌉ rounds are enough.

The last phase is the query. Two binary searches find the ends of the block, and every occurrence is
inside it, found without ever scanning the text.

### Boyer-Moore

Every matcher above reads the pattern **left to right**. Boyer-Moore reads it **right to left**, and
that single reversal is the whole idea: a mismatch at the far end of the pattern tells you something
about characters you have not read at all, and lets you skip past them.

It is the only matcher on this page that can be **sublinear** — on a long pattern over a large
alphabet it does not look at most of the text — and it is what `grep` actually does.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="boyer-moore" size={16} />

<ComplexityCard id="boyer-moore">
  **Two rules decide the jump, and the algorithm takes the larger.**

The **bad-character** rule looks at the text character that caused the mismatch. It has a last
occurrence in the pattern, `λ[c]` — that is the second aux row — so slide the pattern until they
line up: `j − λ[c]`. If the character does not occur in the pattern at all, `λ` is 0 and the whole
pattern jumps clear of it, which is where the big skips come from. A large alphabet makes that the
common case, which is why this rule does most of the work on English and little of it on DNA.

The **good-suffix** rule looks at what did match. The suffix `P[j+1‥m]` matched the text, so any
shift that could work must line that suffix up with a place the pattern could hold it. `γ[j]` is how
far to slide before that is possible, and it is written as the corner note on each pattern
character, so at the moment of a mismatch the shift it allows is right there on screen.

**Why both?** The bad-character rule can suggest a shift of zero, or a negative one — that happens
whenever the bad character occurs to the _right_ of the mismatch in the pattern — and a matcher that
took it would loop or go backwards. Watch for the step whose narration says so. The good-suffix rule
is always at least 1, so the maximum of the two is always a real advance.

**What this page checks is stronger than the answer.** Every one of the five matchers here is
verified against the same shared substring search, so an agreement proves only that this input was
handled correctly. Boyer-Moore is additionally checked for the property both heuristics exist to
preserve: **no shift ever steps over an occurrence.** Every jump the run takes is recorded, and
every position it skipped is tested. A wrong γ that happened to skip nothing on this text would slip
past an answer check and not past this one.

**Compare the tapes and the comparison counts.** Set every matcher on this page to the same `n` and
look at the counters. The naive matcher's comparison count is roughly the length of the text; this
one's is a fraction of it, and the gap widens with the pattern. That is the only algorithm here that
gets _faster_ as the pattern gets longer.

</ComplexityCard>
