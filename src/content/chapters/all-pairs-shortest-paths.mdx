Chapter 22 answered "how far is everything from **this** vertex". This chapter answers it for every
starting point at once, and the answer is a matrix rather than a list.

The obvious approach is to run a single-source algorithm V times, and it is not a bad one — §23.3
is exactly that, made to work. But the problem has structure of its own that a repeated
single-source algorithm cannot see, and the first two sections are about exploiting it.

This is also where Part VI meets chapter 14. Both algorithms below are dynamic programs over a
table, and the tables are the same shape as §14.2's. Only the recurrence differs.

## §23.1 — Shortest paths are a matrix product

Write `l^(m)_ij` for the weight of the shortest path from i to j using **at most m edges**. Then

```
l^(m)_ij = min over k of ( l^(m−1)_ik + w_kj )
```

Now put that beside the definition of a matrix product:

```
c_ij     = sum over k of ( a_ik · b_kj )
```

They are the same expression with `min` in place of `sum` and `+` in place of `×`. Extending
shortest paths by one edge **is** a matrix multiplication, in an arithmetic where "adding" means
taking the smaller and "multiplying" means adding.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="apsp-matrix-multiply" size={4} />

A shortest path has at most n − 1 edges, so the answer is W multiplied by itself n − 1 times, which
is Θ(V⁴) and worse than useless. **The value of the observation is that this product is
associative** — so it can be computed by repeated squaring. L², L⁴, L⁸, and you reach L^(n−1) in
⌈lg(n−1)⌉ multiplications rather than n − 1.

Θ(V³ lg V), from noticing an analogy. Watch the top block become the bottom block and the number of
edges paths may use **double** rather than increase by one.

Compare this player with chapter 4's matrix multiplication side by side. The inner loop is the same
row-against-column sweep; only the two operations changed. That substitution — swapping one pair of
operations for another in a fixed algorithm — is a pattern this chapter uses twice more.

## §23.2 — Floyd-Warshall

A different decomposition, and a better one. Instead of asking how many edges a path may use, ask
**which vertices it may pass through**, and add them one at a time.

Let `d^(k)_ij` be the shortest path from i to j whose intermediate vertices all come from
`{1, …, k}`. Adding vertex k to the allowed set leaves exactly two possibilities: either the best
path still avoids k, or it uses k, in which case it splits into i→k and k→j and both halves are
already known.

```
d^(k)_ij = min( d^(k−1)_ij , d^(k−1)_ik + d^(k−1)_kj )
```

<AlgorithmPlayer id="floyd-warshall" size={4} />

Three lines, Θ(V³), and no auxiliary structure at all.

**Watch the pivot row and column**, which the run marks on every step. An entry can only improve by
combining one cell from row k with one from column k, so everything that happens in a round happens
through the cross those two make. Entries _in_ row k and column k do not change during round k —
that small lemma is what makes updating the table in place legal, and it is why the algorithm needs
no second matrix despite the recurrence naming `D^(k−1)` on the right-hand side.

**The outer loop is over k**, and it must be. The loop variable is not a position in the matrix; it
is how much freedom paths have been granted. Permute the loops and the algorithm is simply wrong —
which is the most common way to get Floyd-Warshall wrong from memory.

Negative edges are fine. A negative _cycle_ shows up as a negative number on the diagonal: a path
from a vertex back to itself costing less than nothing. That is the cheapest negative-cycle test in
the book.

## §23.2 — Transitive closure

Now the substitution again. Replace `min` with **or** and `+` with **and**:

```
t^(k)_ij = t^(k−1)_ij  or  ( t^(k−1)_ik  and  t^(k−1)_kj )
```

<AlgorithmPlayer id="transitive-closure" size={5} />

Same loops, same order, same Θ(V³) — and it answers "can i reach j at all", with no weights
involved.

The picture is different to watch. Distances shrink gradually and it is never obvious when an entry
has settled; reachability only ever **turns on**, so the table fills in one direction and never goes
back.

The practical difference is that booleans pack. A row of `t` fits in machine words, so the inner
loop becomes a bitwise OR over Θ(V/w) words rather than Θ(V) separate operations — the same
asymptotic bound and a large constant factor that no version of the distance table can have.

Both pairs of operations, `(min, +)` and `(or, and)`, form what algebraists call a **semiring**, and
Floyd-Warshall works over any of them. Swap in `(max, min)` and you get widest paths; swap in
regular-expression union and concatenation and you get Kleene's algorithm for converting an
automaton to a regular expression. One triple loop, many problems.

## §23.3 — Johnson's algorithm

Floyd-Warshall is Θ(V³) whether the graph has a million edges or ten. On a **sparse** graph that is
wasteful: V runs of Dijkstra would cost O(V² lg V + V E), which is much better.

The obstacle is that Dijkstra requires non-negative weights, and this chapter exists because of
graphs that have negative ones.

**So change the weights.** Give every vertex a number `h(v)` and reweight every edge:

```
ŵ(u, v) = w(u, v) + h(u) − h(v)
```

<AlgorithmPlayer id="johnson" size={5} />

Two properties make this exactly right, and both are visible in the run.

**It preserves shortest paths.** Along any path the h terms telescope — every intermediate vertex
contributes `+h` once and `−h` once — so all that survives is `h(start) − h(end)`. Every path
between the same two vertices changes by the **same** amount, so the shortest one stays shortest.
That is why the correction at the end is a single subtraction rather than a recomputation.

**It can be made non-negative.** Take `h(v)` to be the shortest distance from a new vertex joined to
everything with weight 0. Then `h(v) ≤ h(u) + w(u,v)` for every edge — which is just the triangle
inequality, exactly what §22.5 proves — and that rearranges to `ŵ(u,v) ≥ 0`.

Watch the edge labels change. Every negative weight goes to zero or above, and nothing about which
path is shortest moves. The added vertex stays on screen after the reweighting, doing nothing: its
only job was to be a source that reaches everything.

The V runs of Dijkstra afterwards are chapter 22's player, so they are summarised one source at a
time here rather than stepped through again.

<ComplexityCard id="floyd-warshall">
  **Density decides.** Floyd-Warshall is Θ(V³) regardless; Johnson is O(V² lg V + V E). With
  E ≈ V² they are the same, and every edge you remove favours Johnson. With E ≈ V, Johnson is a
  factor of V/lg V faster.

**Floyd-Warshall wins on everything else.** Three lines, no priority queue, no auxiliary graph, and
a constant factor so small that on anything under a few hundred vertices it is the right choice
whatever the density.

**All four here handle negative weights**, and all four detect a negative cycle — Floyd-Warshall by
a negative diagonal entry, Johnson by the Bellman-Ford pass failing before anything else runs.

</ComplexityCard>

## Comparing them

Open the **trace tape** — the strip under the transport — on Floyd-Warshall and then on the matrix
squaring player.

Floyd-Warshall's is uniform: V³ steps, all the same shape, with improvements scattered through and
thinning out as the rounds go on. The squaring player's is blockier — each entry of the square is
one step regardless of the graph — and there are far fewer of them, because ⌈lg(n−1)⌉ rounds is two
where Floyd-Warshall does four. That gap goes the other way as V grows: lg V rounds of V³ work
against V rounds of V² work.

The tape is drawing the difference between Θ(V³ lg V) and Θ(V³) — and showing that on a small graph
the better bound is the slower algorithm.

## What this chapter is really about

Three of the four algorithms here are the **same triple loop** with something swapped: min-plus,
or-and, and Floyd-Warshall's own combination of them. The fourth makes a hostile input into a
friendly one so that an algorithm you already have can be used unchanged.

Both moves are more general than the chapter. Recognising that your problem is an existing one over
a different arithmetic, or that a transformation makes it into an existing one, is most of what
algorithm design turns out to be — and it is the thing chapter 34's reductions make formal.
