Every algorithm in this book so far has had one running time. A parallel one has **two**, and
almost everything in chapter 26 follows from the gap between them.

**Work, T₁** — the total number of steps, which is what a single processor would take. Erase every
`spawn` and `sync` from a parallel program and what is left is an ordinary serial program, its
**serial elision**; the work is that program's running time. Going parallel does not make work
disappear. It usually adds a little.

**Span, T∞** — the longest chain of steps that _must_ happen in order, which is what infinitely many
processors would take. Nothing can outrun its own dependencies.

Their ratio, **T₁/T∞, is the parallelism**: the greatest speedup any number of processors could ever
give you. Two processors on a computation with parallelism 1.5 will not help much, and two thousand
will help exactly as little.

Both numbers are well defined because a fork-join computation is a **directed acyclic graph**.
Vertices are _strands_ — runs of code with no parallel control in them — and edges say "this must
finish before that starts". Work is the number of vertices. Span is the longest path. Everything
else in the chapter is a statement about that graph.

## The vocabulary, in three keywords

`spawn` says the called procedure **may** run in parallel with the caller, which continues
immediately. It is permission, not instruction; a scheduler is free to ignore it.

`sync` says the caller waits here until everything it spawned has finished.

`parallel for` says all the iterations may run at once. It is not primitive — a compiler turns it
into a balanced binary tree of spawns, which costs Θ(lg n) span and some extra work.

Because `spawn` is only permission, the serial elision is always a legal execution. That is a
remarkably useful property: **a correct parallel program is one whose serial elision is correct**,
and you can debug it on one processor. It holds only if the program is free of **determinacy races**
— two strands touching the same location with at least one writing — which is why §26.1 spends as
long on races as it does on speedup.

## §26.1 — The DAG, and the two numbers

<AlgorithmPlayer id="p-fib" size={5} />

P-FIB is deliberately a bad algorithm: exponential work to compute what a loop does in linear time.
That is exactly why it is the example. Three lines generate an enormous DAG, which is what you need
in order to see the shape of one.

The whole tree is on screen from the first frame, so nothing moves as it runs. Watch two phases.
The first lights it up in the order **one** processor would execute it — that is the work, one
vertex at a time, and there is a great deal of it. The second traces a single path from the root
down: that is the span, and it is all that stands between this computation and finishing instantly.

**Work is the area of the picture. Span is its depth.** Every technique in the chapter is an attempt
to keep the first large and the second small.

The numbers are exact rather than asymptotic. An invocation with n ≤ 1 is one strand; any other is
three — the code before the spawn, the continuation that calls P-FIB(n−2), and the code after the
sync. Push the slider and watch the ratio move:

```
n        2     3     4     5     6
T₁       5     9    17    29    49
T∞       4     6     8    10    12
T₁/T∞  1.3   1.5   2.1   2.9   4.1
```

Work grows like φⁿ and span like n, so parallelism grows very fast — but at n = 5 it is 2.9, and a
third processor would already be mostly idle. Asymptotic parallelism is a promise about large
inputs, and the small print is the same as everywhere else in the book.

### What you actually get from P processors

You will not get T₁/T∞. You get, from a **greedy scheduler** — one that never leaves a processor idle
while there is work ready — a guarantee that is much better than it has any right to be:

```
T_P  ≤  T₁/P + T∞
```

That is within a factor of 2 of optimal, because no schedule can beat either `T₁/P` (the **work
law** — P processors do at most P steps per unit time) or `T∞` (the **span law**). So the greedy
schedule is at most the sum of two things, each of which is a lower bound.

The practical reading is the ratio **T₁/(P·T∞)**, the _parallel slackness_. When it is comfortably
above 1, the `T₁/P` term dominates and you get near-linear speedup. When it approaches 1, you are
span-bound and adding processors stops helping. That single number is what to compute before buying
a bigger machine.

## §26.2 — Matrix multiplication, in n steps instead of n³

Now the same triple loop as chapter 4, with two words changed: the outer two `for`s become
`parallel for`.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="p-matrix-multiply" size={4} />

Nothing about _what_ is computed differs. The work counter still reaches n³, because every one of
those multiply-adds still happens. What changed is that `c(i, j)` depends on no other entry of C, so
all n² of them can be computed at once, and the only thing left that must happen in order is the
k-loop inside each. Span Θ(n), parallelism Θ(n²).

**Each step of this trace is one step of parallel time.** Watch a column of A and a row of B light
up together: between them they hold exactly one term for every entry of C, and all n² entries take
that term simultaneously. The product is built in n passes.

The clearest way to feel this is to run the two players side by side. Open <a href={chapterHref('divide-and-conquer')}>chapter 4</a>, push its slider to 4, and step its
matrix multiply; then step this one. Same size, same work, same product: **97 steps there, 7 here.**

That is not the whole story, and §26.2 says so. The span here is Θ(n) rather than Θ(lg n) because
the sum over k is still serial. Doing the sum as a parallel reduction — or using the recursive
divide-and-conquer version that spawns eight sub-multiplications — gets the span down to Θ(lg n) and
the parallelism up to Θ(n³/lg n). The price is temporary space, which is the recurring trade in this
chapter.

## §26.3 — Merging, which does not look divisible at all

Merge sort should parallelise beautifully and does not. Spawning the two recursive sorts is easy;
the problem is `MERGE` itself, which is a left-to-right scan in which every step depends on the one
before. Its span is Θ(n), so the whole sort is stuck at Θ(n) span and parallelism Θ(lg n), no matter
how many processors you own. Hardly worth the trouble.

So §26.3 replaces the merge. The replacement is genuinely surprising, because two sorted lists look
like the last thing in the world you could split.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="p-merge" size={9} />

**Take the median of the longer run, and binary-search for it in the other.** That single choice
splits _both_ runs at once: everything left of both split points is below the median, everything
right is above. So the median's final position is known immediately — it is just the count of
elements below it — and what remains is two smaller merges that share nothing.

**The bracket over A is the subproblem.** Every call owns a contiguous slice of the output and fills
it without ever consulting another call, and that non-overlap is the entire licence to run them at
the same time. Watch the bracket shrink.

Why the median of the _longer_ run? Because that is what bounds the shrinking. The longer run holds
at least half the elements, so halving it leaves each side with at most **3/4** of what the call
started with — even in the worst case, where the binary search sends the whole of the shorter run
one way. Depth Θ(lg n), span Θ(lg² n), and merge sort as a whole reaches Θ(lg³ n) span with
parallelism Θ(n/lg² n). The player's `verify` asserts that 3/4 on every recursive call the run
makes, rather than quoting it: an implementation that halved the _shorter_ run would still merge
perfectly and would quietly have a linear span.

P-MERGE-SORT itself is not a separate player here, because it is <a href={chapterHref('getting-started')}>chapter 2's merge sort</a> with `spawn` in front of one recursive call and this merge in place of the other. The interesting half is on this page.

<ComplexityCard id="p-merge">
  **Work is conserved; span is what you design for.** Every algorithm in this chapter does the same
  number of operations as its serial version, sometimes a few more. The whole art is arranging for
  as few of them as possible to be forced into a sequence.

**Parallelism is a ratio, and ratios have small print.** T₁/T∞ is an upper bound on speedup at every
number of processors. What decides whether a real machine gets near it is the slackness T₁/(P·T∞),
and that depends on your input size, not just your algorithm.

**The serial elision is the specification.** A race-free fork-join program computes exactly what its
spawn-free version computes, which means correctness can be reasoned about serially and only
performance needs the DAG. Determinacy races are what forfeit that, and they are the reason this
chapter's model is worth having.

</ComplexityCard>

## Where else this shows up

The fork-join model here is not a textbook abstraction — it is Cilk, and it is what OpenMP tasks,
Intel TBB, Java's ForkJoinPool, Rust's Rayon and Go's goroutine-plus-WaitGroup idiom all implement.
The work-stealing scheduler underneath them is what delivers the `T₁/P + T∞` bound in practice.

Work and span also explain a result you may have met from the other direction. A GPU has thousands
of processors and helps enormously with matrix multiplication, whose parallelism is Θ(n²), and not
at all with a linear scan, whose span equals its work. That is not a fact about hardware. It is
the span law.

And the habit generalizes past processors. Any time work is split across machines, requests or
people, the same two numbers apply: how much there is to do, and how long the longest unavoidable
chain of dependencies is. The second one is the critical path on a project plan, and it is why
adding people to a late project makes it later.
