Every analysis so far has asked what one operation costs. This chapter asks what a **sequence** of
them costs, and the answer is repeatedly much better than the sum of the worst cases.

That gap is not a technicality. Multiply the worst case by n and you get a bound that is true,
provable, and wrong by a factor of n — which would mean rejecting a structure that is in fact the
right one. Amortized analysis is how you get the honest number.

One thing to be clear about first, because the word invites the wrong reading. **Amortized analysis
has no probability in it.** Chapter 5 averaged over random choices, and its results were expected
values that a bad run could miss. Here there is no randomness and no distribution over inputs: the
guarantee is that _every_ sequence of n operations costs what is claimed, in the worst case. What is
averaged is the cost across the operations of the sequence, not across possible worlds.

## §16.1 — Aggregate analysis

The first method is also the bluntest: work out the worst-case cost `T(n)` of **the whole
sequence**, then divide by n. Every operation gets the same amortized cost `T(n)/n`, even when the
operations are of different kinds and obviously differ in price.

### Stack operations

Take a stack with a third operation, `MULTIPOP(S, k)`, which pops the top k objects — or empties
the stack, if it holds fewer than k. Its cost is the number of pops it performs, so a single
`MULTIPOP` on a deep stack costs Θ(n), and the naive bound for n operations is Θ(n²).

That bound is never attained, and the reason is almost too simple to feel like a proof: **each
object can be popped only once, and it must be pushed before it can be popped.** So over any
sequence of n operations the number of pops cannot exceed the number of pushes, which cannot exceed
n. Total cost at most 2n; amortized cost per operation at most 2, which is O(1).

<AlgorithmPlayer id="multipop" size={12} />

The sequence is a mix of pushes and multipops — a positive number pushes it, and `−k` calls
`MULTIPOP(S, k)`. Step to any `MULTIPOP` and watch the bracket collapse: that operation is
genuinely expensive, and the strip above the stack counts what it cost.

Now watch what it takes to _make_ one expensive. The bracket can only collapse by as much as it has
previously grown, one push at a time, and each of those pushes was an operation of its own. An
expensive `MULTIPOP` is not an exception to the cheap operations — it is **paid for by them**, and
it cannot happen without them. The running total on the right of that strip is the evidence: let
the sequence finish and it will be at most twice the number of operations, whatever mix you give
it.

The other thing to notice is one chapter 10 already made: popping does not erase. The values stay
in the array outside the bracket, and a later push overwrites one. `MULTIPOP` moves a boundary k
times; that is all it ever does.

### Incrementing a binary counter

The second example is a k-bit counter, `A[0‥k−1]`, with `A[0]` the low-order bit. `INCREMENT` clears
the run of 1s at the bottom and sets the first 0 above them, so its cost is the number of bits it
flips: 1 for an even counter, and k in the worst case, when every bit is 1.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="binary-counter" size={12} />

The bits are drawn as we write numbers, low-order at the right, and the bracket over the right-hand
end is the price of the increment shown _before_ it is paid: every bit under it will be flipped.
Watch it as the run proceeds. Most increments are bracketed over one bit. Every second one is
bracketed over at least two, every fourth over at least three, and the wide brackets are exactly as
rare as they are expensive.

That is the whole argument, and it is worth writing down as arithmetic. `A[0]` flips on every
increment. `A[1]` flips on every second one. `A[i]` flips ⌊n/2^i⌋ times. So the total over n
increments is

> n + n/2 + n/4 + … < 2n

whatever the sequence looks like — a geometric series that does not depend on k at all. The
`writes` counter under the visualization is the running total of flips, and if you let the run
finish you will find it below twice the number of increments, every time. It is not an observation:
the module's own correctness check asserts it on every input the site generates.

## §16.2 — The accounting method

The second method makes the sharing explicit. Assign each _kind_ of operation an **amortized cost**
that may differ from what it actually costs. When the amortized cost is more than the real one, the
difference is stored as **credit** on a specific object in the structure; when it is less, the
stored credit pays the difference.

The one rule: **the total credit must never go negative**, for any sequence and at any point. If it
never does, the amortized costs sum to an upper bound on the true cost, because you have only ever
spent money you had already collected.

For the stack:

| Operation      | Actual cost | Charged |
| -------------- | ----------- | ------- |
| `PUSH`         | 1           | 2       |
| `POP`          | 1           | 0       |
| `MULTIPOP(k′)` | k′          | 0       |

A push is charged two: one for doing the push, and one left sitting **on the object itself** as
credit that will pay for its own eventual pop. Every object on the stack carries exactly one unit,
so the credit in the structure is never negative, and any pop — however deep, however many at once
— is already funded. The total charge for n operations is at most 2n.

The same accounting works on the counter. Charge 2 to set a bit: one for the write, and one left on
that bit to pay for clearing it later. Clearing is then free, and since a bit must be set before it
can be cleared, the credit on the counter is the number of 1s in it, which is never negative. Each
`INCREMENT` sets at most one bit, so it is charged at most 2.

## §16.3 — The potential method

The third method stores the same prepayment in the structure as a whole rather than on individual
objects. A **potential function** Φ maps a state of the structure to a number, and the amortized
cost of an operation is

> ĉᵢ = cᵢ + Φ(Dᵢ) − Φ(Dᵢ₋₁)

— what it really cost, plus the change in potential it caused. Sum that over the sequence and the
middle terms telescope, leaving the total actual cost plus Φ(Dₙ) − Φ(D₀). So if Φ starts at zero and
never goes below it, the amortized costs are an upper bound on the real ones.

Go back to the `MULTIPOP` player and look at the strip above the stack, which is this equation
being kept live. **Take Φ to be the number of objects on the stack — which is exactly the width of
the bracket.** A push does one unit of work and raises Φ by one, so ĉ = 1 + 1 = 2. A `MULTIPOP` of
k′ objects does k′ units of work and lowers Φ by k′, so ĉ = k′ − k′ = 0. Expensive operations are
precisely the ones that release potential, and the strip shows ĉ landing on 2 or 0 no matter how
much that particular step cost.

For the counter, take Φ to be **the number of 1s**. An increment that clears t ones and sets one bit
does t + 1 units of work and changes the potential by 1 − t, so

> ĉ = (t + 1) + (1 − t) = 2

for every increment, whatever t is. The t cancels — which is the thing to remember about the
potential method, because that cancellation is what makes it feel like a trick the first few times.
It is not: Φ was chosen so that a run of 1s is stored-up potential, and clearing that run spends it.

The three methods are three ways of writing the same argument, and which one to reach for is a
matter of what the structure makes easy. Aggregate is the shortest when you can bound the whole
sequence directly; accounting is natural when the prepayment belongs to identifiable objects;
potential is the most flexible, and it is the one the rest of the book uses.

## §16.4 — Dynamic tables

Chapter 10 left a real problem unsolved. Every array-based structure there had a capacity fixed
when it was created, and a stack that outgrows its array has nowhere to put the next element.

The fix is to allocate a bigger table and copy everything across. The only question is how much
bigger, and it is the difference between a good structure and a useless one. **Grow by one slot**
and each of the n insertions copies everything before it: 1 + 2 + … + (n−1), which is Θ(n²) — worse
than useless, since you could have sorted the data in that time. **Double**, and the picture
changes completely.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="dynamic-table" size={10} />

The bracket here is `T.size`, the table **as actually allocated**; the outlines beyond it are room
the drawing keeps so nothing rescales mid-run, not slots the table owns. The first insertion allocates a single slot; after that, watch the
bracket double at insertions 2, 3, 5 and 9 — and then not again until 17. Each expansion copies twice as much as
the one before it and happens half as often, and those two factors cancel exactly.

Most insertions do one write. The expensive ones are the insertions that land on a full table, and
they cost Θ(T.num) because every item has to be copied into the new block. (The drawing keeps the
items in place; in memory they have all moved to a fresh allocation, which is precisely what the
copying step is.)

Add it up. Over n insertions the copies come to 1 + 2 + 4 + … , a geometric series that stays under
n, so the total work is under 2n and the amortized cost per insertion is a constant. Doing it with
the potential method takes one line: let

> Φ(T) = 2 · T.num − T.size

which is 0 just after an expansion and rises to T.num by the time the table is full again — so the
expansion always finds exactly the potential it needs. Then ĉ = 3 for **every** insertion, cheap or
expensive.

<ComplexityCard id="dynamic-table">
  **The amortized cost is 3 and the worst case is still Θ(n)** — both are true, and neither replaces
  the other. If you are writing a server that must answer within a deadline, the Θ(n) is the number
  that matters, and this structure will occasionally miss. If you care about throughput over many
  operations, the 3 is the number that matters, and it is exact.

**The `2 · T.num − T.size` potential is worth reading as a sentence**: each item inserted since the
last expansion carries two units, one for its own eventual copy and one for an item from the older
half that has no credit of its own. That is the accounting method wearing the potential method's
clothes.

</ComplexityCard>

### Deleting, and the mistake to avoid

Insertion alone would let a table grow without ever shrinking, so the natural companion is: halve
the table when it becomes half empty. **This is wrong**, and instructively so. Sit exactly at the
boundary and alternate an insertion with a deletion: each insertion finds the table full and
doubles it, each deletion drops it to half full and halves it, and every single operation copies
the entire contents. Θ(n) amortized, on a structure that is supposed to be Θ(1).

The fix is to leave a gap between the two triggers: **halve when the table falls to one quarter
full**, not one half. After a contraction the table is half empty, so a great many operations must
happen before either trigger fires again, and the amortized cost goes back to a constant. The
lesson generalises beyond tables — a structure that resizes at the same point it grows and shrinks
will thrash, and the cure is hysteresis.

## Look at the two tapes

Set the counter and the table to the same `n` and compare their tapes — the strip beside the
transport, one tick per step, marked by what that step did. The two patterns are the same shape:
long stretches of cheap operations, punctuated by bursts of writes, and each burst is twice as long
and half as frequent as the one before it. On the counter the bursts are carries running up through
the bits; on the table they are expansions copying everything across.

That self-similarity is what amortized analysis is really about. Neither structure is cheap on
every operation, and neither needs to be: what makes both of them O(1) is that the expensive
operations are rationed by the cheap ones that came before them.

## Where this gets used

Chapter 11 needed this chapter and could not have it yet. A hash table's guarantees all assume the
load factor α stays below some constant, which means the table has to grow — and now you know what
that costs. It is the same doubling, with the added detail that every key has to be **rehashed**
rather than copied, because `h` depends on `m`.

Chapter 19 will lean on it much harder. The disjoint-set forests there have a running time nobody
would guess from a single operation, and it is amortized over the sequence. The potential method is
how you get there.
