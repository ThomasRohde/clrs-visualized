Heapsort gets the best of both worlds: the Θ(n log n) guarantee of merge sort and the O(1) space of
insertion sort. It manages this with a data structure that exists only implicitly — a binary tree
whose parent-child links are arithmetic on array indices rather than pointers.

## The array is the tree

For a node at index `i`, its children live at `2i` and `2i+1` and its parent at `⌊i/2⌋`. No
pointers, no allocation — the tree is just a way of reading the array. The **max-heap property**
says every parent is at least as large as its children, so the maximum is always sitting at `A[1]`.

## Two phases

Heapsort runs in two distinct phases, and the dashed line in the visualization marks the
boundary between them. First `BUILD-MAX-HEAP` imposes the heap property on the whole array. Then
the sorting loop repeatedly swaps `A[1]` — the maximum — into its final position at the end, shrinks
the heap by one, and calls `MAX-HEAPIFY` to restore the property at the root.

Watch the settled region grow leftward from the right edge: that is the sorted tail, and once a
bar squares off it never moves again.

<ComplexityCard id="heapsort">
  **Why BUILD-MAX-HEAP is only O(n):** `MAX-HEAPIFY` costs O(log n) and `BUILD-MAX-HEAP` calls it
  n/2 times, so the obvious bound is O(n log n). But that bound is loose. The real cost depends on
  node height, and a heap is overwhelmingly bottom-heavy: half its nodes are leaves that need no
  work at all, a quarter sit one level up, and so on. Summing height × count over all levels gives
  a series that converges to O(n).

The n−1 extractions that follow are genuinely O(log n) each, so the overall cost is Θ(n log n) —
the build phase is not the bottleneck.

**Not stable:** the swap in the sorting loop moves elements across long distances, so equal keys
do not keep their original relative order.

</ComplexityCard>

## §6.5 — Keeping the heap instead of consuming it

Heapsort builds a heap and then destroys it. A **max-priority queue** keeps it: the same array,
the same `MAX-HEAPIFY`, but now it is a live structure you can query, drain and refill. Four
operations are enough.

`HEAP-MAXIMUM` is the reason to bother. The largest key is always at `A[1]`, so asking for it is
one array read and no work at all — Θ(1), against Θ(n) for an unsorted array.

`HEAP-EXTRACT-MAX` takes that maximum out. It moves the last leaf up to the root, shrinks
`heap-size` by one, and calls `MAX-HEAPIFY` to let the new root sink to wherever it belongs. This
is the same move heapsort's inner loop makes, which is not a coincidence: heapsort is exactly
`BUILD-MAX-HEAP` followed by n−1 extractions.

`HEAP-INCREASE-KEY` runs the other way. Raising a key can only break the heap property upwards, so
the element climbs, exchanging with its parent until the parent is at least as large. Nothing below
it can be disturbed, which is why one path to the root — O(log n) — is enough.

`MAX-HEAP-INSERT` is a trick rather than a new procedure: extend the heap by one slot, put −∞ in
it, and then raise that placeholder to the key you actually wanted. Since −∞ is smaller than
everything, the raise is always legal, and `HEAP-INCREASE-KEY` does the real work.

<ComplexityCard id="max-priority-queue">
  **Every operation is one root-to-leaf path.** A heap on n elements has height ⌊log n⌋, and
  `MAX-HEAPIFY` and the climb inside `HEAP-INCREASE-KEY` each walk at most one such path. That is
  the whole analysis; there is no amortization and no averaging hidden in it.

**What a priority queue is not.** It will not give you the second-largest key in Θ(1), or list its
contents in order without emptying itself. A heap is much weaker than a sorted array — and that
weakness is what makes insertion O(log n) instead of O(n).

**Where this gets used later.** Prim's minimum-spanning-tree algorithm and Dijkstra's
shortest-paths algorithm are both loops around `EXTRACT-MIN` and `DECREASE-KEY`, the mirror image
of the two operations above. Chapters 21 and 22 do not re-derive any of this; they simply call it.

</ComplexityCard>
