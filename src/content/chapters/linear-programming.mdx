A **linear program** asks for the best value of a linear function subject to linear constraints:

```
maximise    cᵀx
subject to  Ax ≤ b
            x ≥ 0
```

That is all. No products of variables, no conditionals, no integrality — and it is astonishing how
much fits into it anyway.

Geometrically, the constraints carve out a convex polytope in n dimensions and the objective picks a
direction; the answer is the furthest point of the polytope in that direction. Because the region is
convex and the objective is linear, **the optimum is always attained at a vertex**, which is the
fact every algorithm for the problem exploits.

The 4th edition drops the simplex algorithm, so this chapter has no procedure to animate. What it
has instead is a way of thinking, and one theorem that is worth more than most algorithms.

## §29.1 — What a linear program is, and what solving one costs

There are three outcomes: an optimal solution, an unbounded objective, or an empty feasible region.
Real formulations hit the last two more often than you would expect, and usually because the model
was wrong rather than because the problem was.

Two families of algorithm exist and they are strange mirror images.

**Simplex** walks from vertex to vertex along the edges of the polytope, always improving. It is
exponential in the worst case — Klee–Minty's cube forces it to visit all 2ⁿ vertices — and it is what
almost everybody actually runs, because on real instances it takes a small number of steps.

**Interior-point methods** cut through the middle of the polytope instead and are provably
polynomial. They are the reason "linear programming is in P" is a true statement, and they are
competitive in practice on large sparse problems.

That an exponential-worst-case algorithm is the practical default and a polynomial one is the
theoretical answer is a good corrective to reading complexity classes as advice. It is the same
lesson <a href={chapterHref('quicksort')}>chapter 7</a> teaches with quicksort, at a larger scale.

## §29.2 — Formulating problems as linear programs

The skill this chapter is really teaching is recognising a linear program when it is not written as
one. Several problems that already have their own chapters are LPs underneath:

**Maximum flow** is immediate. One variable per edge, capacity constraints on each, conservation at
each vertex, maximise what leaves the source. <a href={chapterHref('maximum-flow')}>Chapter 24</a>
solves it with a combinatorial algorithm because that is faster, not because it has to.

**Shortest paths** is an LP too, and its shape is instructive: _maximise_ d(t) subject to
`d(v) ≤ d(u) + w(u,v)` for every edge and `d(s) = 0`. The constraints are exactly the triangle
inequality that <a href={chapterHref('single-source-shortest-paths')}>chapter 22</a>'s relaxation
step enforces, and maximising is right because the constraints push the estimates _down_.

**Minimum-cost flow, assignment, and multicommodity flow** are LPs with no comparably fast
combinatorial algorithm, which is when you actually reach for a solver.

And one that is not: **integer** linear programming, where the variables must be whole numbers, is
NP-hard. That single added requirement moves the problem from P to the far side of <a href={chapterHref('np-completeness')}>chapter 34</a>, which is as sharp a demonstration as the
book offers that difficulty lives in the details of a specification.

The gap between an integer program and its **relaxation** — the same program with integrality
dropped — is exactly the lever <a href={chapterHref('approximation-algorithms')}>§35.4</a> pulls: solve the easy relaxation, round
the fractional answer, and bound how much the rounding cost you.

## §29.3 — Duality, which is the reason to read the chapter

Every linear program has a **dual**, obtained by transposing the problem: the primal's constraints
become the dual's variables and vice versa.

```
primal:   maximise cᵀx   s.t.  Ax ≤ b,   x ≥ 0
dual:     minimise bᵀy   s.t.  Aᵀy ≥ c,  y ≥ 0
```

**Weak duality** says any feasible dual solution is an upper bound on any feasible primal one. That
is easy to prove and immediately useful: it means a dual solution is a **certificate**. You can hand
someone a number and a reason nobody can do better, and they can check it without redoing your work.

**Strong duality** says the two optima are _equal_. That is not obvious and it is the theorem.

Once you have it, several results elsewhere in the book stop being separate facts:

- **Max-flow min-cut** is the primal-dual pair for flow. The minimum cut is the dual solution, which
  is why <a href={chapterHref('maximum-flow')}>chapter 24</a>'s algorithm can prove its flow optimal
  by exhibiting a cut rather than by searching further.
- **König's theorem** — in a bipartite graph, the maximum matching equals the minimum vertex cover —
  is the same duality restricted to a 0/1 case, and is why
  <a href={chapterHref('bipartite-matching')}>chapter 25</a>'s matching comes with a proof attached.
- The **Hungarian algorithm**'s potentials are a dual solution, and the reason that player can
  certify its assignment is optimal with a single sum rather than by trying all n! of them.

The habit worth taking from this chapter is that one. **When an algorithm can prove its own answer
optimal, there is usually a dual hiding in it** — and finding it turns a program you have to trust
into one that hands you a receipt.

## Where this shows up

Solvers are a commodity now, and the practical consequence is that the expensive skill is modelling
rather than solving: getting a real problem into the form above, and knowing when it will not go.
Production planning, portfolio allocation, network design, diet and blending problems, scheduling
under resource limits — all of them are LPs when the quantities are continuous and integer programs
the moment something has to be a whole number.

The other reason to know this chapter is defensive. When you meet an optimisation problem in the
wild, "is this a linear program?" is a cheap question with a very large payoff if the answer is yes,
and a useful one if the answer is "yes, but with integrality" — because that tells you immediately
which of the last two chapters of the book to read next.
