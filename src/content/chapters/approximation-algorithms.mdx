Chapter 34 ends badly. A long list of problems people actually need solved turns out to be
NP-complete, and unless P = NP none of them has a polynomial-time algorithm. That is a proof, not a
gap in anyone's cleverness, and no amount of engineering will get around it.

But the problems do not go away. Crews still have to be scheduled, trucks still have to be routed.
So the question changes: **if you cannot have the best answer, what can you have?**

Three things are on the table, and you must give up one of them: optimality, speed, or generality.
This chapter gives up the first, and — this is what makes it a chapter rather than a shrug —
gives it up by a **measured amount**.

An algorithm is a **ρ(n)-approximation** if, on every input of size n, the answer it returns is
within a factor of ρ(n) of the optimal one:

```
max( C / C*, C* / C ) ≤ ρ(n)
```

The ratio is written to work either way round, so the same definition covers minimizing a cost and
maximizing a profit. ρ = 1 means exact; larger ρ means worse. And ρ is a **guarantee**, proved in
advance against an optimum C\* that nobody computed and nobody can compute.

That last point is what makes this chapter strange and worth reading slowly. Every proof here
compares an algorithm's output against a quantity that is, by assumption, unobtainable. The trick,
every time, is to find something you _can_ see that is provably no bigger than the optimum — a
matching, a spanning tree, a harmonic sum — and squeeze the answer between them.

Every player on this page does exactly that, and then checks it. Each one's `verify` computes the
true optimum by brute force and asserts the chapter's bound on every input the page generates. That
is only possible because the instances here are tiny, which is itself the point: brute force is
precisely what these algorithms exist to avoid.

## §35.1 — Vertex cover

A **vertex cover** is a set of vertices touching every edge; find the smallest. This is NP-hard, and
the algorithm for it is close to the simplest thing that could possibly work.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="approx-vertex-cover" size={9} />

Take any uncovered edge. Put **both** endpoints in the cover. Delete every edge either of them
touches. Repeat.

Taking both ends is obviously wasteful — one of them would have sufficed for that edge — and the
waste is exactly what buys the proof. Because every picked edge has both its ends removed from play,
**no two picked edges share a vertex**: they form a matching, drawn in the mark colour and left on
screen for the whole run.

Now the squeeze. Any vertex cover at all, including the optimal one, must contain at least one
endpoint of every matching edge, so `C* ≥ |M|`. This algorithm returns exactly `2|M|`. Therefore

```
|C| = 2|M| ≤ 2·C*
```

and the factor of two is proved without ever knowing what C\* is.

The matching, in other words, is not a by-product. It is the certificate. Count those edges on
screen, double the number, and you are holding both the answer and a proof of its quality.

**This bound is not pessimistic.** On randomly generated graphs this player lands at about 1.84×
optimal and frequently hits 2.00 exactly — unlike everything else on this page, the worst case here
is the ordinary case, and you will see it in a few clicks of Randomize.

It is worth knowing what does _not_ help. The obvious improvement — repeatedly take the vertex of
highest degree — feels far smarter and is strictly worse: its ratio grows as Θ(lg V). Being greedy
about a plausible-looking quantity can cost you more than not being clever at all.

## §35.2 — The travelling salesperson

Visit every city once, return home, spend as little as possible. Here the news is worse than
NP-hardness: §35.2 proves that for the general problem, **no constant-factor approximation exists at
all** unless P = NP. A polynomial ρ-approximation for any constant ρ could be used to decide whether
a graph has a Hamiltonian cycle, which is itself NP-complete.

So the section adds one assumption, and the picture changes completely. Require the **triangle
inequality** — going direct is never worse than going via somewhere else — and there is a
two-line algorithm within a factor of 2.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="approx-tsp-tour" size={8} />

**Build a minimum spanning tree, then walk it.** A preorder walk lists the cities in the order the
tree first reaches them; visit them in that order and go home. Chapter 21's algorithm, used as a
subroutine, and nothing else.

Two inequalities, and both are on the screen.

**w(T) ≤ C\*.** Delete any single edge from an optimal tour and what remains is a spanning tree.
A spanning tree cannot be cheaper than the minimum one. So the tree the player builds — a thing you
can watch being built — is a lower bound on an optimum nobody computed.

**c(H) ≤ 2·w(T).** A full walk of the tree crosses every edge exactly twice, so it costs `2w(T)`.
The tour is that walk with the repeat visits skipped, and the triangle inequality says a shortcut
is never longer than the path it replaces.

Together, `c(H) ≤ 2w(T) ≤ 2·C*`.

The second phase is where that assumption gets spent, and it is visible. As the walk runs, the tree
stays on screen dashed and the tour is drawn solid over it. Most tour edges follow a tree edge
downwards; then the walk finishes a branch and has to get back, and the next tour edge **leaps
across the picture** to a city the tree reaches by a long way round. Every one of those leaps is a
use of the triangle inequality. Without it, that edge could be arbitrarily expensive and the whole
argument collapses — which is precisely why the general problem admits no constant factor.

Cities here are points in the plane and cost is distance, rounded **up**. The ceiling is not
fussiness: `⌈a⌉ + ⌈b⌉ ≥ a + b ≥ c`, and the left-hand side is a whole number, so it is at least
`⌈c⌉`. Ordinary rounding can lose the triangle inequality by 1 on three nearly collinear cities, and
the guarantee would go with it. Type your own cities in and the metric still holds by construction —
which is also why the box asks for **points** rather than an edge list. A human typing 45 pairwise
costs would violate the assumption within about four of them.

In practice this player runs at about **1.17× optimal**, and the worst case seen in a few hundred
random instances is 1.36 — comfortably inside the promised 2, which is the usual relationship
between a worst-case bound and a typical input. Christofides' refinement, the same tree plus a
minimum matching on its odd-degree vertices, brings the guarantee down to 3/2.

## §35.3 — Set covering

You have a universe X of things that must be covered and a family F of subsets you may buy; use as
few as possible. This is the abstract form of an enormous number of real problems — which crews to
hire, which servers to site, which tests to run in a suite — and it generalizes vertex cover.

The greedy rule is the one anybody would try first: take whichever set covers the most
still-uncovered elements, and repeat.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="greedy-set-cover" size={10} />

**The right-hand column is the algorithm.** It holds `|S ∩ U|` for every set — what each one is
worth _right now_ — and the whole of line 4 is "take the largest number in that column". Watch the
column after each pick: a set that looked excellent a moment ago can be worth nothing once another
set has taken its elements, and greedy re-reads the whole column every round precisely because those
numbers move.

The ratio is **H(d)**, the d-th harmonic number, where d is the size of the largest set — at most
`ln|X| + 1`. That is a much weaker promise than the previous two sections' constants, and it is the
best available: no polynomial-time algorithm beats `ln|X|`, up to lower-order terms, unless P = NP.
Greedy here is not a stopgap on the way to something better. It is the answer.

On random instances greedy usually finds the optimum outright — it fell short on about 9% of the
ones this generator produces. To see it fail, type this into the box:

```
1,2,3,4; 1,2,5; 3,4,6
```

Greedy takes S₁ first, because four is more than three. That leaves 5 and 6 stranded in different
sets, so it needs both, and finishes with three. But S₂ and S₃ cover everything between them. The
mistake is visible at the very first step and greedy cannot see it, because seeing it would require
knowing what the _rest_ of the run will need — which is the same blindness chapter 27's online
algorithms have, arrived at from the opposite direction.

## §35.4 — Randomization, and linear programming

Two general techniques rather than two algorithms, which is why this section is prose here.

**Randomization.** For MAX-3-CNF satisfiability — satisfy as many three-literal clauses as you can
— set every variable to true or false by a **coin flip**. A clause fails only if all three of its
literals come out wrong, which happens with probability 1/8, so each clause is satisfied with
probability 7/8 and by linearity of expectation you satisfy 7/8 of them in expectation. That is a
randomized 8/7-approximation, and it does not look at the input at all. The lesson is the one
chapter 7 taught about quicksort: an adversary can defeat any fixed choice, and cannot defeat a coin.

**Linear programming.** For weighted vertex cover, write the problem as an integer program — one
0/1 variable per vertex, one constraint per edge — and then _drop_ the integrality requirement.
The resulting linear program is solvable in polynomial time, and its optimum is a lower bound on the
integer one, since every integer solution is also a fractional one. Then **round**: take every
vertex whose fractional value reached 1/2. Each edge's constraint `x(u) + x(v) ≥ 1` guarantees at
least one of its ends clears that bar, so the rounded set is a cover, and no vertex's weight is
inflated by more than a factor of 2.

Relax, solve, round. It is the most productive single recipe in the field, and the lower bound it
leans on is the same kind of object as §35.1's matching and §35.2's spanning tree: something
computable that provably sits below the optimum.

Chapter 29 covers the linear programs themselves; the 4th edition drops the simplex algorithm, so
there is no solver to animate here.

## §35.5 — Subset sum, and dialling in the accuracy

Given a set of numbers and a target t, find the subset summing as close to t as possible without
going over. The exact algorithm is easy to write and hopeless to run: keep the list of every sum you
can reach, and each new number doubles it.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="approx-subset-sum" size={5} />

One idea fixes it. **If two reachable sums are within a hair of each other, keep only one.**
Trimming by a factor of `1 + δ` collapses the list to `O(ln t / δ)` entries however many numbers
there are, and the error introduced compounds by at most `1 + δ` per round. Choose `δ = ε/2n` and
the run comes back within a factor of `1 − ε` of the true best.

Three things happen to L every round, and the row's length tells the whole story:

- **it doubles**, because every sum plus xᵢ is also reachable;
- **it is trimmed**, which is the only reason it does not explode — and the only place accuracy is
  lost;
- **it is cut at t**, since a sum over the target can never come back under it.

The running time is polynomial in n **and** in 1/ε. That makes this a **fully polynomial-time
approximation scheme**, the strongest thing in the chapter and the only place in the book where you
get to _dial_ how good an answer you want and pay a predictable, published price for it. Halving ε
doubles the list, and so doubles the work.

ε is fixed at 0.4 here, matching the book's own worked example — type `308; 104, 102, 201, 101` to
run it. The guarantee at that setting is only that the answer is within 40% of the best, and yet in
practice the player lands at about **97.7% of optimal**, with the worst case over a few hundred runs
at 92.5%. The bound is not a prediction. It is a promise about an adversary who is not there.

<ComplexityCard id="approx-subset-sum">
  **A ratio is a promise, not an estimate.** Every bound on this page is a worst case over all
  inputs, and three of the four players beat theirs comfortably on ordinary ones. Vertex cover is
  the exception worth remembering: its factor of 2 is routinely achieved, so there the guarantee
  really is a description.

**The proof always needs a visible lower bound.** A matching, a spanning tree, a harmonic sum, an
LP relaxation. You cannot compare against the optimum directly — the whole premise is that you
cannot have it — so every argument here finds a computable quantity that provably sits below it and
squeezes the answer between the two.

**The assumptions are load-bearing.** TSP has no constant-factor approximation without the triangle
inequality; it has a 2-approximation with it. When an approximation guarantee is quoted at you,
the first question is what it assumed.

</ComplexityCard>

## Where else this shows up

This is the chapter that comes closest to describing what most working software actually does. A
scheduler packing jobs onto machines, a compiler allocating registers, a delivery service planning
routes, a cloud provider placing containers — all of these are NP-hard in their exact form and all
of them ship anyway, running something with a ratio attached.

Chapter 27's online algorithms are the same bargain struck against a different obstacle. There the
input is not hard to process, it is merely not all there yet; here it is all present and the
difficulty is that computing the optimum would take longer than the universe has left. Both settle
for a provable factor, both prove it against an optimum they cannot reach, and both are honest
about the gap in the same way.

The habit is worth taking away from the book whole. When a problem turns out to be intractable, the
useful next question is not "is there a clever trick" but **"what can I prove about how close I can
get, and what does that proof cost me?"**
