A network of pipes, each with a capacity. One **source** producing material, one **sink** consuming
it, and every other junction obliged to pass on exactly what it receives. How much can get through?

The question is older than computing and turns up wherever something is routed through a shared
network with limits — traffic, bandwidth, water, electricity, freight — and, less obviously, in a
long list of problems that are not about flow at all. Chapter 25 is one of them.

## §24.1 — Flows and cuts

A **flow** assigns each edge a number between zero and its capacity, such that every vertex except
the source and the sink sends out exactly what comes in. The **value** of the flow is the net amount
leaving the source. Maximising it is the problem.

The other half of the chapter is the thing that bounds it. A **cut** splits the vertices into two
groups with the source on one side and the sink on the other; its **capacity** is the total capacity
of the edges crossing from the source's side to the sink's. Every unit of flow has to cross every
cut somewhere, so:

**No flow can exceed the capacity of any cut.**

That is the easy direction and it is immediate. The hard direction — that some flow actually
achieves some cut's capacity — is the **max-flow min-cut theorem**, and it falls out of the
algorithm below rather than needing a separate proof.

## §24.2 — The Ford-Fulkerson method

It is called a _method_ rather than an algorithm, and the book means it: it says what to do and not
how. The how is the second half of this same section.

Push flow along any path from source to sink that still has room. Repeat until there is no such
path.

Try that naively and it is simply wrong. A first path can commit flow to an edge in a way that
blocks two better paths later, and with no way to take it back the answer comes out too small. What
fixes it is one idea.

**The residual network.** After some flow has been pushed, the useful question is not "which edges
have spare capacity" but "which changes are still available" — and there are two kinds. An edge
carrying 3 of 5 can take 2 more. An edge carrying 3 can also give **3 back**: sending flow the other
way along it cancels what is there, which is a perfectly legal change and often the necessary one.

Each edge is labelled with its flow over its capacity. The **dashed arrows are residual edges** —
they are not pipes, they are permissions to undo — and they appear and disappear as flow moves,
because a residual edge only exists while there is flow to cancel. Watch for a path that uses one:
the narration says so, and it is the algorithm reversing an earlier decision without any backtracking
machinery at all.

**When it stops, it is optimal, and the reason is on the screen.** When no augmenting path is left,
the vertices still reachable from the source in the residual network form one side of a cut. Every
edge leaving that set must be full — or the search could have crossed it — and every edge entering
it must be empty. So the flow equals that cut's capacity exactly. Since no flow can exceed any cut,
both are optimal, and the ring at the end of the run is the minimum cut.

That is the max-flow min-cut theorem, and it arrives as a side effect of the stopping condition.

## §24.2, continued — Edmonds-Karp

The method's cost depends entirely on how the path is chosen, which is the thing it declines to
specify. Choose badly and each augmentation can add a single unit of flow, so the running time
depends on the **capacities** — the numbers written on the edges — rather than on the size of the
graph. With capacities in the millions, that is a million iterations on a graph with four vertices.
With irrational capacities the method can fail to terminate at all.

Fix it by pinning down one detail: **always take a shortest augmenting path**, counting edges and
ignoring capacities entirely. Find it with a breadth-first search.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="edmonds-karp" size={8} />

The search is visible here in a way it was not above: the ring grows outward one distance at a time,
exactly as in §20.2, and the path it returns is the shortest one available in the residual network.
The narration names its length each round.

**Watch that number.** It never decreases, and that is the lemma the whole bound rests on. Augmenting
along a shortest path can only remove edges from the current level structure or add ones pointing
backwards, and neither can bring a vertex closer to the source. Each augmentation also **saturates**
at least one edge on its path, and a saturated edge cannot reappear on an augmenting path until the
distance to one of its ends has strictly increased. Distances only go up, and only as far as |V| —
so each edge can be the bottleneck at most |V|/2 times, giving at most V·E/2 augmentations in total.

Both claims are checked on every generated input: that path lengths never decrease, and that the
augmentation count stays inside V·E/2. Neither is true of an arbitrary path choice, which is what
makes them the test that this is Edmonds-Karp and not the generic method.

## §24.3 — Maximum bipartite matching

The chapter's last section is the first payoff, and it is not about networks at all.

Given a bipartite graph, add a source joined to every left vertex, a sink joined from every right
vertex, and give **every edge capacity 1**. A maximum flow in that network is a maximum matching:
the unit capacities let each vertex carry at most one unit, so the edges with flow on them share no
endpoints, and the value of the flow is the number of pairs.

The detail that makes the reduction exact rather than approximate is the **integrality theorem**:
with integer capacities every augmentation adds an integer amount, so no edge ends up carrying half
a unit — which would be half a pair and meaningless.

Chapter 25 takes the reduction apart again and runs the matching directly, without the source, the
sink or the capacities — so the player for it lives there rather than here.

## Comparing them

Run both on the same network with the **trace tape** open — the strip under the transport.

Ford-Fulkerson's tape shows longer, more irregular augmentations: the depth-first search dives, so
it often returns a winding path, and the flow it can push is limited by whatever narrow edge that
path happened to include. Edmonds-Karp's is regular — a visible breadth-first sweep, then a short
path, then a sweep again — and the paths get longer in steps as the near routes fill up.

They agree on the answer, always, because both stop only when the residual network cannot reach the
sink and that condition is what proves optimality. What they disagree about is how many rounds it
takes, and the step counter under each player is the honest measure of that.

<ComplexityCard id="edmonds-karp">
  **The two players are the same algorithm with one line specified differently**, and the difference
  in the bound is the difference between a running time that depends on the numbers in the input and
  one that depends only on its size.

**O(V E²) is not the last word.** Dinic's algorithm augments along all shortest paths of a given
length at once and reaches O(V² E); the push-relabel family, in §26.4 of the third edition and in
this chapter's problems, does better still. All of them are refinements of the same residual-network
idea.

**Integer capacities give an integer maximum flow.** Every augmentation adds an integer amount, so
the flow never becomes fractional — which is what makes chapter 25's reduction work at all.

</ComplexityCard>

## Why this chapter is worth more than it looks

Maximum flow is the reduction target for a surprising number of problems that are not about
networks. Bipartite matching is the next chapter and is exactly this with every capacity set to one.
Scheduling with resource constraints, image segmentation, project selection, and the assignment of
people to tasks all become flow problems with the right graph in front of them.

The pattern is worth naming, because it recurs: rather than inventing an algorithm for your problem,
find the shape it already has.
