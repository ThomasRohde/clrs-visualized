Dynamic programming has the worst name in computer science. It is not dynamic and it is not
programming; Richard Bellman picked the words in the 1950s partly because they were impossible to
object to in a funding proposal.

What it actually is: **solve each subproblem once, and write the answer down.**

That is the whole technique. Everything else in this chapter is about recognising when it applies,
and working out what to keep in the table.

The tables below are not illustrations of these algorithms. They **are** the algorithms — the entire
computation is the order the cells get filled in and where each one looks to. That is the one thing
worth carrying out of the chapter: when a dynamic program is hard to understand, draw its table and
the difficulty usually turns out to have been about the order, not the idea.

## §14.1 — Rod cutting

A rod of length n can be cut into pieces; a piece of length i sells for `p[i]`. What is the rod
worth?

The recursive answer is immediate and useless. Try every possible first cut, and solve the remainder
the same way — obviously correct, and Θ(2ⁿ), because it re-solves the same remainder over and over.
A rod of length 40 takes longer than the exercise is worth.

Nothing about the recursion is wrong. What is wrong is doing it twice.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="rod-cutting" size={8} />

Fill a table instead: solve length 1, then length 2, and so on, so that by the time a subproblem is
needed its answer is already sitting there. Θ(2ⁿ) becomes Θ(n²), and the code is if anything simpler.

Watch each new entry take **one price from the top row and one revenue from a cell already filled**.
Those two arrows are the recurrence, and the reason the whole thing is quadratic is that there are n
entries and each looks at at most n such pairs.

The number under each revenue is `s[j]`: **the length of the first piece** in a best cutting of that
length. It costs one extra write per cell, and it is the difference between knowing what the rod is
worth and knowing where to cut it. The run reconstructs the cuts at the end by following those
stored choices back — a walk the table was built to make possible.

## §14.3 — When does this work?

Two properties, and they are what you actually check when deciding whether a problem is a dynamic
program.

**Optimal substructure.** An optimal solution contains optimal solutions to its subproblems. This
sounds like it must always be true and is not: the longest _simple_ path between two vertices does
not have this property, because the two halves of a longest path can share vertices, and gluing two
optimal halves together may not give a path at all. Shortest paths have it. Longest paths do not —
and that difference is exactly why chapter 22 is easy and longest-path is NP-hard.

**Overlapping subproblems.** The same subproblem comes up again and again. Without this, there is
nothing to gain by remembering: merge sort's subproblems are all distinct, which is why it is
divide-and-conquer and not dynamic programming.

A third practical matter, and the one the chapter spends most time on: **the table needs a fill
order** in which every entry's dependencies are already there when it is reached. In §14.1 that is
left to right; in the next two sections it is up the diagonals, which is less obvious and is the
only fiddly part of either.

**Top-down memoization is the same algorithm.** Write the natural recursion, and cache each answer
the first time it is computed. It fills exactly the same entries, usually a little slower for the
recursion overhead, and it has one real advantage: it only touches the subproblems actually needed.
Where a bottom-up table computes entries nothing ever reads, memoization skips them.

## §14.2 — Matrix-chain multiplication

Matrix multiplication is associative, so `A(BC)` and `(AB)C` give the same answer — and can cost
wildly different amounts of arithmetic. Multiplying a `p × q` matrix by a `q × r` one costs `p·q·r`
scalar multiplications, so where the parentheses go decides how much work the chain takes. The
book's example differs by a factor of ten.

The number of parenthesisations is a Catalan number, Ω(4ⁿ/n^{3/2}), so trying them all is out.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="matrix-chain-order" size={6} />

Whatever the best parenthesisation is, it splits somewhere: it multiplies some `Aᵢ‥A_k` by some
`A_{k+1}‥A_j` at the top, and both halves must themselves be optimal. So the recurrence tries every
split point and takes the cheapest.

**The fill order is the thing to watch.** Entries are computed by chain _length_, moving up the
diagonals, because `m[i, j]` needs answers for shorter chains inside it. The two arrows on each step
are the two subchains — one running left along the row, one running down the column — and watching
them sweep across the row as `k` moves is watching the min being taken.

The bottom half of the table stays empty, and should: `m[i, j]` means nothing when `i > j`. The
diagonal is zero, because one matrix on its own takes no multiplications at all.

## §14.4 — Longest common subsequence

A **subsequence** keeps the order but not the adjacency: `BCDB` is a subsequence of `ABCBDAB`,
because you can strike letters out and be left with it. The longest one two sequences share measures
how alike they are — and it is the measure `diff` uses, so every file comparison you have ever read
is this table.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="lcs" size={7} />

The recurrence comes from looking at the last letter of each sequence. If they match, that letter is
in some longest common subsequence and the rest of the answer is the LCS of everything before both.
If they do not, at least one of the two is not in the answer, so take the better of dropping one or
dropping the other.

Three cases, three places an entry can come from, and the arrow in each cell records which. **A
diagonal arrow means the letters matched and the count went up; up or left means one sequence gave
up a letter and the count stayed the same.** Following the arrows back from the bottom-right corner
spells out an actual subsequence, which is what the run does at the end.

Note what the corner cell alone tells you: the _length_. Recovering the subsequence itself is why
the arrows are stored. This is the same trade as `s[j]` in §14.1 and `s[i, j]` in §14.2, and it is
the general shape — **a dynamic program computes a number, and a second table records the choice
that produced it.**

## §14.5 — Optimal binary search trees

Chapter 12 built search trees and chapter 13 kept them balanced. Balance minimises the **worst**
search. This section minimises the **average** one, and the answer is often a deliberately
unbalanced tree.

If you know how often each key is looked for, the best tree puts popular keys near the root even at
the cost of pushing rare ones deep. A key at depth d costs d + 1 comparisons, so a tree's expected
cost is the sum over every key of frequency × depth, plus the same for the **unsuccessful** searches
— the gaps between keys, which is where a search for a key that is not there ends up. Ignore the
gaps and you get the wrong tree.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="optimal-bst" size={5} />

The recurrence has §14.2's shape, for the same reason: whatever the optimal tree is, some key is its
root, and its two subtrees must themselves be optimal. So try every key as the root of every stretch.

The term worth understanding is the one added to both subtree costs. When a subtree gains a parent,
**every node in it goes one level deeper**, so its cost rises by the total frequency of everything
inside it. That is `w(i, j)`, and it is why the two subtree costs cannot simply be added together.

The note in each cell is the root that achieves that cost. Following those roots recursively builds
the actual tree, which is what the run prints at the end.

<ComplexityCard id="matrix-chain-order">
  **Count subproblems, then multiply by the work each one costs.** That is the whole running-time
  analysis for every dynamic program in the chapter: rod cutting has Θ(n) subproblems at O(n) each;
  matrix chains and optimal trees have Θ(n²) at O(n) each; LCS has Θ(mn) at O(1) each.

**The space is usually reducible and the reconstruction usually is not.** LCS needs only two rows to
compute the length, but recovering the subsequence needs the whole table of choices — which is the
trade every one of these makes.

**Memoization gets the same bound**, and only touches subproblems that are actually reached.
Bottom-up wins on constants; top-down wins on sparse dependency structures.

</ComplexityCard>

## Comparing the four

Open the **trace tape** — the strip under the transport — on rod cutting and then on matrix-chain
order, and the difference in shape is the difference in the recurrence.

Rod cutting's tape is a steadily lengthening sawtooth: each new entry scans one more candidate than
the last. Matrix-chain order's is a series of shorter and shorter bursts, because the diagonals get
shorter as they get longer — there are n − 1 chains of length 2 and only one of length n. LCS's tape
is almost flat, because every entry costs the same O(1). The tape is drawing the sum that gives each
one its running time.
