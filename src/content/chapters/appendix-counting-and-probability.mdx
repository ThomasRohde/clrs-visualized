Randomized algorithms and average-case analysis both cash out into counting, and this appendix is
the toolkit. It is also where **linearity of expectation** lives, which is the single most useful
technique in the book's analytical chapters.

## §C.1 — Counting

```
strings of length k over an alphabet of n      nᵏ
permutations of n items                        n!
ordered k-subsets of n                         n!/(n − k)!
unordered k-subsets of n  ("n choose k")       C(n,k) = n!/(k!(n − k)!)
all subsets of n                               2ⁿ
```

The two identities used most often:

**C(n,k) = C(n−1,k−1) + C(n−1,k)** — Pascal's rule, and the reason binomial coefficients have a
dynamic-programming flavour: either the n-th item is in the subset or it is not.

**C(n,k) ≤ (en/k)ᵏ**, and **n! ≈ √(2πn)(n/e)ⁿ** (Stirling). The second gives **lg(n!) = Θ(n lg n)**,
which is the whole of <a href={chapterHref('sorting-in-linear-time')}>chapter 8</a>'s lower bound: a
comparison sort's decision tree needs n! leaves, so it needs height lg(n!) = Θ(n lg n), so merge sort
is asymptotically optimal and no cleverness will improve it.

## §C.2 — Probability

A sample space, events as subsets, a probability distribution. Two rules do nearly everything:

`Pr{A ∪ B} = Pr{A} + Pr{B} − Pr{A ∩ B}`, and the **union bound** `Pr{∪Aᵢ} ≤ ΣPr{Aᵢ}`, which needs
no independence at all and is therefore usable when nothing else is.

`Pr{A | B} = Pr{A ∩ B} / Pr{B}`, conditional probability, and **independence** when
`Pr{A ∩ B} = Pr{A}·Pr{B}`.

The word to be careful with is _independent_, because analyses quietly assume it and the assumption
is often false. <a href={chapterHref('hash-tables')}>Chapter 11</a> is explicit about this: simple
uniform hashing is an assumption, universal hashing is what you do when you are not willing to make
it, and the difference between the two is the difference between an average-case result and a
guarantee.

## §C.3 — Random variables and expectation

A random variable maps outcomes to numbers; its **expectation** is `E[X] = Σ x·Pr{X = x}`.

And then the identity that matters more than the rest of this appendix combined:

```
E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]        — always, whether or not X and Y are independent
```

**Linearity of expectation** needs no independence. That is what makes it so powerful, because
independence is exactly the thing that is usually missing.

The pattern it enables is worth learning as a pattern, since the book uses it a dozen times:

1. write the quantity you want as a sum of **indicator** random variables — one per event, worth 1 if
   the event happens and 0 otherwise;
2. note that `E[Iₐ] = Pr{A}`;
3. add up the probabilities, ignoring entirely how tangled the dependencies are.

That is <a href={chapterHref('probabilistic-analysis')}>chapter 5</a>'s whole method. The hiring
problem: `E[hires] = Σ 1/i = ln n + O(1)`, because candidate i is hired exactly when it is the best
of the first i. Quicksort's expected comparisons: sum over pairs of the probability that they are
ever compared, which is `2/(j − i + 1)`, and the pairs are wildly dependent — and it does not matter.
Universal hashing's expected chain length falls out the same way.

Also here: **Markov's inequality**, `Pr{X ≥ t} ≤ E[X]/t`, which is the cheapest possible tail bound
and needs only that X is non-negative. It is what turns an expected running time into a statement
about how often you get an unlucky one.

## §C.4 — The distributions that come up

**Bernoulli**: one trial, success with probability p. **Binomial**: the number of successes in n
independent trials, with mean np. **Geometric**: the number of trials until the first success, with
mean 1/p.

The geometric distribution is the one to recognise on sight, because "keep trying until it works"
is a recurring algorithmic shape and its expected cost is always 1/p. It is why a randomized
algorithm that succeeds with probability ½ per attempt costs about two attempts, and why <a href={chapterHref('hash-tables')}>open addressing</a>'s expected probe count is `1/(1 − α)` —
each probe hits an occupied slot with probability about α.

## §C.5 — Tail bounds (★)

The starred section bounds the probability of landing far from the mean. The practical summary:
binomial tails fall **exponentially** in the distance from the mean, which is what makes
"with high probability" statements possible at all.

The consequence worth carrying: a randomized algorithm whose expected cost is good is usually also
_reliably_ good, not merely good on average. That is the real reason <a href={chapterHref('quicksort')}>randomized quicksort</a> is trusted in practice — not that its
average is Θ(n lg n), but that the probability of being much worse than the average shrinks
exponentially, and it does so **on every input**, because the randomness is in the algorithm rather
than in an assumption about the data.
