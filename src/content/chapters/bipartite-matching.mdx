Workers and jobs, each worker qualified for some of the jobs. Students and projects. Doctors and
hospitals. Ports and plugs. In every case the graph is **bipartite** — two groups, with edges only
between them, never inside — and the question is how many pairs can be made at once, with nobody
used twice.

A set of edges no two of which share a vertex is a **matching**. This chapter is about finding a
maximum one.

## §25.1 — Why greedy is not enough

Take any available edge, mark both ends used, repeat. It is the obvious algorithm and it does not
work — not because it is unlucky, but because it can genuinely paint itself into a corner. Match a
worker to the only job two other workers can do, and two matches are lost to gain one.

The natural repair would be to back out and try again, and the natural fear is that this means
searching all the possibilities. It does not, and the reason is the same one that rescued the last
chapter.

**An augmenting path** starts at an unmatched vertex, alternates unmatched and matched edges, and
ends at a _different_ unmatched vertex. Flip every edge along it — matched becomes unmatched,
unmatched becomes matched — and since such a path has one more unmatched edge than matched ones, the
matching grows by exactly one. Everything that was matched along the way stays matched; some of it
just changes partner.

**Berge's theorem** is what makes this an algorithm rather than a heuristic:

> A matching is maximum **exactly when** no augmenting path exists.

So there is nothing to search and nothing to reconsider. Look for an augmenting path from each
unmatched vertex on the left, take it if you find one, and when none is left anywhere you are
provably done. The stopping condition **is** the proof of optimality.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="bipartite-matching" size={9} />

The recursion in the player is the alternation. Trying to match `u` to `v`, and finding `v` already
taken, the only way forward is to ask `v`'s current partner to go somewhere else — which is the same
question one level down. When that chain of requests reaches somebody with a free option, the whole
chain shifts by one and the matching is one bigger. When it does not, the search unwinds and `u` is
left unmatched.

Watch what happens to a vertex that fails. It is never retried. Once no augmenting path exists from
a vertex, none ever will — later augmentations cannot create one — so a failed search is a permanent
answer, which is what keeps the running time to O(VE) instead of something that restarts.

## The reduction to maximum flow

This is chapter 24 wearing a disguise, and the disguise is thin.

Add a source joined to every vertex on the left, a sink joined from every vertex on the right, and
give **every edge capacity 1**. Now a maximum flow is a maximum matching: the unit capacities mean
each left vertex can send at most one unit and each right vertex can receive at most one, so the
edges carrying flow form a matching, and its size is the value of the flow.

Two details make the reduction exact rather than approximate. **Integer capacities give an integer
maximum flow**, so no edge ends up carrying half a unit — which would be a matching of half a pair
and meaningless. And the augmenting paths of §24.2 become precisely the augmenting paths above, with
the residual edges — the permissions to undo — turning into the matched edges you traverse backwards.

The algorithm on this page is that reduction with the source, the sink and the capacities removed,
because none of them ever did anything. It is worth doing once in each direction: understanding the
matching version makes the flow version's residual edges concrete, and understanding the flow
version explains why the matching one is guaranteed to terminate at the right answer.

<ComplexityCard id="bipartite-matching">
  **O(VE) is one search per unmatched left vertex**, each costing a walk over the edges. Since the
  matching grows by at most one per search and can never shrink, there are at most |V| searches.

**König's theorem** is the companion result: in a bipartite graph the size of a maximum matching
equals the size of a minimum vertex cover. That is max-flow min-cut again, translated — the cut in
the flow network is the cover.

**Hopcroft-Karp** (still §25.1) improves this to O(E √V) by finding a **maximal set of shortest
augmenting paths** in each phase rather than one path at a time, in the same way Dinic's algorithm
improves Edmonds-Karp. It is the standard algorithm in practice, and there is a player for it at
the end of this chapter.

</ComplexityCard>

## §25.2 — The stable-marriage problem

A different question about the same kind of graph, and the answer is not "the most pairs" but "no
pair that would both rather defect".

Each of n people on one side ranks all n on the other, and vice versa. A matching is **unstable** if
some pair not matched to each other would both prefer each other to their current partners — they
would leave, and the matching would not hold. A **stable** matching admits no such pair.

**Gale-Shapley** finds one, and always.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="gale-shapley" size={8} />

Unmatched proposers propose down their own preference list; a receiver holds the best offer so far
and rejects the rest; anyone rejected proposes to their next choice. Only proposals actually made
are drawn, so the picture is the history of the round as well as its state — a dashed edge is an
offer that was made and is no longer held.

Three claims, each with a one-line argument:

- **It terminates**, because nobody ever proposes twice to the same person: at most n² proposals.
- **Everyone ends up matched.** A receiver who has ever held an offer never becomes free again. So a
  leftover proposer would have been rejected by all n receivers, all of whom would then be matched —
  which needs n + 1 proposers.
- **It is stable.** If `p` prefers `r` to their own partner, then `p` proposed to `r` earlier and was
  turned down or later dropped, and that only happens in favour of somebody `r` prefers. So `r` does
  not want `p`, and the pair does not defect.

The badge on each vertex is how far down their own list their current partner sits — watch the
proposers' numbers get worse as they work down, and the receivers' get better as offers arrive.

It is also lopsided in a way worth knowing: the algorithm is **optimal for whichever side
proposes** and pessimal for the other. Every proposer gets the best partner they could have in any
stable matching; every receiver gets the worst. That asymmetry is not a bug in the algorithm, it is
a property of the problem, and it is the reason real deployments — medical residency matching, for
one — argue about which side proposes.

## §25.3 — The Hungarian algorithm

The weighted version, also called the **assignment problem**: every pair has a value, and the goal
is the best total rather than the most pairs.

Augmenting paths alone are not enough here: a matching can be maximum and expensive. Trying every
assignment is n!, so what makes a polynomial algorithm possible is **linear-programming duality**,
in the concrete form of a price on every row and every column.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="hungarian" size={4} />

Give each worker a potential `u[i]` and each job a potential `v[j]`, kept **feasible** — meaning
`u[i] + v[j] ≤ c[i][j]` for every pair. A pair where that holds with **equality** is _tight_, and
those are the only pairs the algorithm ever matches. The note in each cell is its **slack**,
`c[i][j] − u[i] − v[j]`; a zero is a tight pair.

Why restricting to tight pairs is enough is the duality argument, and it is short. For _any_
feasible potentials and _any_ complete assignment, the assignment's cost is at least Σu + Σv. So if
a complete assignment can be built out of tight pairs alone, its cost is **exactly** Σu + Σv — and
nothing can be cheaper. The certificate arrives with the answer, which is why the last step of the
run reports both numbers and they agree.

The algorithm alternates two moves. Grow an alternating path inside the tight pairs, exactly as
§25.1 does. When it gets stuck, raise the potentials by the smallest slack on the frontier: watch a
whole row's slacks change at once, feasibility hold everywhere, and at least one new pair go tight —
which is what lets the search continue. One worker is placed per round.

### Hopcroft-Karp — augmenting in phases

The player at the top of this page does one thing per search: find an augmenting path, flip it, and
start again. Each search costs O(E) and there can be V/2 of them, so O(V·E).

Look at what it wastes. A single search explores much of the graph and then uses **one** path out of
it. Hopcroft-Karp uses all the disjoint ones it can find, and that turns out to be worth an entire
factor of √V.

A **phase** is two passes. First a breadth-first sweep from every unmatched left vertex at once,
assigning each left vertex a level and stopping at the layer where a free right vertex first appears
— so the length of the shortest augmenting path is known before any of them is walked. Then a
depth-first pass that walks only along the layers, collecting a **maximal set of vertex-disjoint**
shortest paths, and augments along every one of them together.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="hopcroft-karp" size={10} />

<ComplexityCard id="hopcroft-karp">
  **Two lines do all the work, and neither is the augmentation.** In `DFS-AUGMENT`, the test
  `level[M[v]] == level[u] + 1` is what keeps every path *shortest* — the walk may only descend one
  layer at a time, so it cannot wander into a longer route. And `level[u] = ∞` on the way out is
  what keeps the paths **disjoint**: once a vertex has been walked through and failed, or been used,
  no later path in this phase can reach it. Between them, a whole phase costs O(E) rather than O(E)
  per path.

**Why there are only O(√V) phases.** The claim is that the shortest augmenting path gets **strictly
longer** every phase, and it follows from the set being maximal: if an augmenting path of the old
length survived the augmentation, it would have to be disjoint from the set that was used, and then
the set was not maximal. So after √V phases the shortest path is longer than √V edges. Now count the
other way: a matching that is k short of maximum admits k vertex-disjoint augmenting paths, and if
each is longer than √V they cannot all fit in V vertices unless k < √V. So at most √V improvements
remain, and each takes at most one more phase. Two halves, √V each.

**That strict increase is what this page checks**, on every generated input, rather than the O(√V)
bound itself. At ten vertices the bound is not discriminating — a lazy one-path-per-phase
implementation would satisfy it comfortably — but such an implementation would repeat a path length,
and it would be caught. The matching itself is checked by Berge's theorem, exactly as the first
player's is.

**Watch two things.** The **badges** are levels, and only left vertices carry one: the search only
branches there, since a right vertex is crossed to reach its partner and nothing else. And the
**ring** is the set of still-unmatched vertices, which is what a phase exists to shrink — when it
empties, or when a whole phase finds no path at all, the algorithm is done for the same reason
§25.1's is.

**Compare the tapes.** Set both this player and the one at the top of the page to the same `n` and
look at the two strips. The first is one long uniform run of searching; this one is visibly grouped,
and there are only two or three groups. That grouping is the algorithm.

</ComplexityCard>

## Where Part VI ends

Six chapters, and the shape of them is one idea getting steadily more capable.

Chapter 20 explored a graph. Chapter 21 explored it cheapest-edge-first and got a spanning tree.
Chapter 22 explored it cheapest-path-first and got distances. Chapter 24 explored a graph that
changed as it was explored, and got a maximum flow. This chapter used that on a graph that was not
about flow at all.

Every one of them is a search over a frontier. What changes is what "cheapest" means, and what the
frontier is allowed to reach.
