An **algorithm** is a procedure that takes some value as input, does something well defined, and
produces some value as output. It is **correct** if, for every legal input, it halts with the right
answer.

That definition is short and does real work, because both halves are load-bearing. _For every legal
input_ — an incorrect algorithm may be right on almost everything and still be wrong, and this book
spends a great deal of its effort on the "almost". _Halts_ — an algorithm that is right when it
finishes and sometimes does not finish is not correct, which is why every loop in the book comes
with a reason it terminates.

## §1.1 — Problems, and what makes one hard

A **problem** is a relation between inputs and acceptable outputs. The sorting problem says: given a
sequence, produce a permutation of it in non-decreasing order. That statement mentions no algorithm,
and the distinction matters — the whole of <a href={chapterHref('sorting-in-linear-time')}>chapter 8</a> turns on the difference between a
lower bound for _the sorting problem_ under comparisons and the running time of any _algorithm_ for
it.

Problems come with a shape, and recognising the shape is most of the work. A handful worth having
in mind, all developed later:

- **Sorting and selection.** Rarely the goal; constantly the enabling step. A large fraction of the
  algorithms in this book begin by sorting, and their bound is the sort's.
- **Shortest paths.** In a road network, in a build graph, in a state machine. Chapters 22 and 23.
- **Matching and flow.** Assigning jobs to machines, students to schools, packets to routes.
  Chapters 24 and 25.
- **String matching.** Chapter 32, and everything from a text editor to a genome aligner.
- **Optimisation under constraints.** Chapter 29, and — when the exact answer turns out to be
  unobtainable — chapters 33 and 35.

Two things make a problem hard, and they are not the same thing. Some are hard because there are
**many candidate answers and only a few good ones** — that is what the first two thirds of the book
is about, and the answer is nearly always a structure that lets you avoid looking at most of them.
Some are hard because **no efficient algorithm exists**, which is <a href={chapterHref('np-completeness')}>chapter 34</a>, and the answer is to stop looking and start
approximating.

## §1.2 — Algorithms as a technology

The claim of §1.2 is the one worth arguing with, because it sounds like special pleading from
people who like algorithms, and it is not.

Suppose a fast machine runs insertion sort in optimised assembly at 10 billion instructions per
second, and a machine a hundred times slower runs merge sort in an interpreted language 50 times
less efficient. On ten million numbers, using the constants §1.2 uses:

```
fast machine, insertion sort   2·(10⁷)² instructions / 10¹⁰ per second  ≈  5.6 hours
slow machine, merge sort       50·10⁷·lg(10⁷)  / 10⁸  per second        ≈  20 minutes
```

**The slower computer, running the slower language, wins by a factor of seventeen** — and the gap
widens with every additional element, without limit. There is no version of the hardware story that
catches up, because the two are not competing on the same term.

That is the whole argument, and it generalises past sorting. Hardware and compilers buy you a
constant factor. An algorithm can buy you an exponent, and exponents compound.

The corollary that matters more in practice: **algorithms are what make some things possible at
all.** A route planner over a continent, public-key cryptography, a search engine over the web, an
assembled genome — none of these is a fast version of something people used to do slowly. Each one
exists because somebody found an algorithm, and would not exist otherwise at any clock speed.

## Where this shows up

This chapter has no algorithm to animate, which is the point of it. It is the argument for why the
next thirty-eight chapters are worth reading, and the two habits it asks for run through all of them:

**Say what the problem is before you say what the algorithm is.** Half the difficulty in practice is
that the specification was never written down, and a great many arguments about which algorithm is
better turn out to be arguments about which problem is being solved.

**Count the operations that grow, and ignore the rest.** That is <a href={chapterHref('characterizing-running-times')}>chapter 3</a>'s subject, and it is the tool
that turns "this feels slow" into a claim you can check before writing any code.
