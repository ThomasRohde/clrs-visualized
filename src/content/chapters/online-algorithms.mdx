Every algorithm so far has been handed its whole input before it started. This chapter takes that
away. The input arrives one piece at a time, each decision is **irrevocable**, and the next piece may
be chosen by an adversary who has watched everything you have done.

That changes what "correct" can even mean. There is no optimal answer to compete for — for any
online algorithm there is a sequence that defeats it. So the question is reframed:

> **How much worse than an algorithm that knew the future can you be forced to be?**

An online algorithm is **c-competitive** if its cost is never more than `c` times the optimal
offline cost (plus a constant). That is the chapter's only measure, and it is a genuinely different
kind of guarantee from the rest of the book: not "this is the best", but "**not knowing costs at most
a factor of c**".

## §27.1 — Waiting for an elevator

The chapter opens with a decision anyone has made. You press the button. Do you wait, or take the
stairs? Waiting is free if the elevator arrives soon and wasted if it does not; the stairs cost a
fixed amount but always work. You do not know when the elevator will come.

The strategy that is best in the worst case is **wait exactly as long as the stairs would cost, then
give up**. If the elevator comes first, you win. If it does not, you have spent at most twice what
you would have spent taking the stairs immediately — so the strategy is **2-competitive**, and no
deterministic strategy does better.

That "wait until you have spent what the alternative costs, then switch" rule is worth carrying
around. It is the same argument as rent-versus-buy, as when to abandon a retry loop, and as the
doubling in chapter 16's dynamic table.

## §27.2 — Maintaining a search list

A linked list holding a set, searched from the front. Finding the item at position i costs i, the
requests arrive one at a time, and you may reorder the list as you go. How should you order it?

With the sequence known in advance the answer is trivial: sort by frequency. The point is that it is
not.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="move-to-front" size={9} />

**Move-to-front**: after each access, move the item you just found to the front. That is the entire
algorithm — no counters, no statistics, no memory beyond the list itself. It looks far too crude to
be good, and it is **4-competitive** against any offline algorithm whatsoever.

Watch the bracket. Its width **is** the cost of the access, and keeping it narrow is the only thing
move-to-front is trying to do. It is betting that an item asked for once will be asked for again
soon — **locality of reference**, the same assumption every cache in the world runs on. Type a
uniformly random sequence into the box and watch the bracket stay wide: the bet stops paying, and
nothing else would have done better either.

The proof is a **potential-function** argument, exactly like chapter 16's. Take the potential to be
the number of pairs the two lists disagree about, charge each access its amortised cost, and the
constant falls out. That is the third time in this book that a potential function turns a
hard-to-bound sequence into an easy one.

The player's `verify` computes the true offline optimum by dynamic programming over every reachable
list order — something only possible because the lists here are five items long — and asserts the
factor of four on every generated input.

## §27.3 — Online caching

Now the same problem chapter 15 solved, with the future removed.

§15.4 evicted the block **whose next request is furthest in the future** and proved that unbeatable.
It is also unimplementable: no real cache knows what is coming. So what should one do instead?

<AlgorithmPlayer id="online-caching" size={12} />

**Least-recently-used** is what everything actually runs: evict the block that has gone longest
without being asked for. It is the backwards-looking guess at the forward-looking rule, and it rests
on the same bet move-to-front makes.

**Compare the two brackets.** Chapter 15's points forward along the requests still to come, which is
all its rule consults. This one points backward, over the requests already served, which is all an
online policy is allowed to see. That single difference is the chapter.

LRU is **k-competitive**, where k is the cache size — and that is the best any deterministic online
algorithm can manage. The lower bound is the simplest adversary argument in the book: **always ask
for the block the algorithm just evicted**. Whatever the policy, it misses every single time, while
an offline algorithm with k blocks and the sequence in hand misses only once every k requests.

Randomization escapes it. A policy that evicts a random unmarked block is O(lg k)-competitive
against an _oblivious_ adversary — one who must fix the sequence before seeing any coin flips —
which is the same reason chapter 7's randomized quicksort has no bad input.

## Comparing them

Run this chapter's LRU and chapter 15's offline caching on the **same** request sequence — type the
same numbers into both boxes — and compare the miss counts under each player.

LRU is usually close and never far. The gap you see is the exact price of not knowing the future on
that particular input, and it is almost always much smaller than the factor of k the theorem
allows. That is the honest relationship between a competitive ratio and reality: the bound is a
promise about the worst case, and the worst case has to be constructed on purpose.

<ComplexityCard id="online-caching">
  **Competitive analysis measures regret, not running time.** Both algorithms here are trivially
  cheap per operation; what is being bounded is the quality of their decisions against an opponent
  with perfect information.

**The adversary is the definition.** A competitive ratio is a worst case over all inputs, and for
deterministic online algorithms the adversary can simply simulate you and choose accordingly. That
is why every deterministic caching policy is stuck at k, and why randomization is not a trick here
but the only way out.

**These bounds are pessimistic on purpose.** Real request sequences have locality; adversarial ones
do not. LRU's k-competitiveness is what you are guaranteed, not what you get.

</ComplexityCard>

## Where else this shows up

The online framing is more common than the offline one, once you look for it. A scheduler placing
jobs on machines does not know the jobs still to come. A router forwarding a packet cannot see the
traffic behind it. A load balancer, a memory allocator, an auction accepting bids, a self-driving
car deciding when to brake — all of them commit before the input is complete.

Chapter 35's approximation algorithms are the offline cousin of this idea: there, the whole input is
known and the difficulty is that computing the optimum is intractable. Here the optimum is easy and
the difficulty is that you cannot see it yet. Both settle for a provable factor, and both are
honest about it in the same way.
