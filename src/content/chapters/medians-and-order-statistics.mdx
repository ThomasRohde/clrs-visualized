The i-th **order statistic** of a set is its i-th smallest element. The minimum is the first, the
maximum is the n-th, and the median sits in the middle. Sorting answers all of these at once, in
Θ(n log n) — but if you only want one of them, that is far more work than the question deserves.

This chapter answers a single query in Θ(n), which is optimal: you cannot name the i-th smallest
without at least looking at every element, since any one you skipped might have been it.

## §9.1 — Minimum and maximum

The minimum takes n−1 comparisons: hold a champion, compare it with each remaining element, and
keep the winner. Every comparison eliminates exactly one candidate, and n−1 candidates must be
eliminated, so n−1 is not merely sufficient but necessary — think of it as a knockout tournament in
which every player but one must lose a match.

So the minimum alone is settled. What about the minimum **and** the maximum together?

The obvious answer is two independent scans, at 2n−2 comparisons. The better answer costs only
3⌊n/2⌋, and the trick is to process elements in pairs. Compare the pair against _each other_ first;
then only the smaller of the two can possibly beat the running minimum, and only the larger can
beat the running maximum. Three comparisons for two elements, instead of four.

The player runs both methods, one after the other, over the same array — first the plain

the entire result of this section is in the comparison counter. Note where it stands when

The trace tape is the clearer view. Both passes are pure comparison work, but the second is
visibly shorter for the same array — and it answers twice as many questions.

<ComplexityCard id="minimum-maximum">
  **Where the saving comes from.** In the naive version, each element is compared against both the
  running minimum and the running maximum: two comparisons apiece. Pairing them costs one extra
  comparison per *pair* but then spends only one comparison on each of the two running answers, so
  three comparisons cover two elements rather than four.

**The odd/even setup matters.** With an odd number of elements, the first element initialises both
running answers for free. With an even number, the first pair is compared once to initialise them.
Either way the remaining elements are processed two at a time, and the total lands at 3⌊n/2⌋.

**This bound is tight** — Exercise 9.1-2 asks you to prove that 3⌊n/2⌋ comparisons are also
necessary.

</ComplexityCard>

## §9.2 — Selection in expected linear time

For a general i, the idea comes straight from quicksort — with one change that makes all the
difference.

`RANDOMIZED-SELECT` partitions the array around a randomly chosen pivot, exactly as
`RANDOMIZED-QUICKSORT` does. Partitioning tells it the pivot's rank `k` in the subarray. If `i`
equals `k`, the pivot is the answer. Otherwise the answer lies strictly on one side, and — here is
the change — the other side is **thrown away unsorted**. Quicksort recurses on both halves; select
recurses on one.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="randomized-select" />

The player asks for the lower median, ⌈n/2⌉. The bracket marks the part of the array that might
still hold the answer: everything outside it has been discarded, and will never be examined again,
let alone sorted. Watch the bracket contract by roughly half each time — that shrinkage is the
whole algorithm.

**Compare the tapes** with quicksort's on the same array. Quicksort's tape is one full-width
figure repeated at every level of the recursion, because every element is touched at every level.
Select's is a staircase that halves: n work, then n/2, then n/4. That geometric series sums to less
than 2n, which is why the total is Θ(n) rather than Θ(n log n) — recursing on one side instead of
two changes the answer, not merely the constant.

<ComplexityCard id="randomized-select">
  **T(n) = T(n/2) + Θ(n) solves to Θ(n).** Compare quicksort's T(n) = 2T(n/2) + Θ(n), which solves
  to Θ(n log n). The single difference is the coefficient on the recursive term, and it is the
  difference between a geometric series that converges and one that has log n equal levels.

**Expected, not guaranteed.** A run of unlucky pivots still gives Θ(n²) — partitioning around the
minimum every time peels off one element at a time. As with randomized quicksort, the expectation
is over the algorithm's own coin flips and therefore holds for every input, but it is an
expectation nonetheless.

**In practice this is the one you use.** §9.3 removes the "expected" at a constant-factor cost that
is rarely worth paying.

</ComplexityCard>

## §9.3 — Selection in worst-case linear time

`RANDOMIZED-SELECT` is fast because a random pivot is usually good. `SELECT` is fast because it
refuses to accept a bad one — it spends real work choosing a pivot that is _guaranteed_ to be
roughly central.

The construction is the famous median of medians:

1. Divide the elements into groups of five and sort each group.
2. Take the median of each group.
3. Recursively call `SELECT` to find the median of those medians. Call it `x`.
4. Partition the whole subarray around `x`, and recurse on whichever side holds the answer.

Step 3 is the part that looks circular — `SELECT` calling itself to choose its own pivot — and it
is not, because the recursive call is on n/5 elements rather than n.

The payoff is the guarantee. At least half the group medians are ≤ x, and each of those groups
contributes three elements (its median and the two below it) that are also ≤ x. That is
3·(n/10) ≈ 3n/10 of the array on each side, so no matter what the input is, partitioning around x
discards at least 3/10 of it. The recursion is T(n) ≤ T(n/5) + T(7n/10) + Θ(n), and because
1/5 + 7/10 < 1 that solves to Θ(n) — in the worst case, with no probability involved anywhere.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="select" />

This is the 4th edition's in-place presentation, and the groups of five are **strided**, not
contiguous: group j is `A[j]`, `A[j+g]`, `A[j+2g]`, `A[j+3g]`, `A[j+4g]`. That looks like an
eccentric way to divide the array until you see what it buys — once every group is sorted, each
group's median sits in the middle fifth of the subarray, all of them together, ready to be selected
from with no copying and no extra array. The step that says so is worth pausing on: it is the frame
where all g medians are marked at once, and they are contiguous.

The leading `while` loop is bookkeeping rather than insight. It trims the subarray from the left,
one minimum at a time, until its length is a multiple of five — at most four trips, so Θ(n) total.

<ComplexityCard id="select">
  **The constant factor is the catch.** Sorting every group of five, then recursing to find their
  median, then partitioning, is a great deal of work per level. `SELECT` is linear and
  `RANDOMIZED-SELECT` is only expected-linear, yet in practice the randomized one wins on almost
  every real input. Real implementations use introselect: run the randomized version, and fall back
  to this one only if it is taking too long.

**Why five?** Groups of three do not work: the recurrence becomes T(n/3) + T(2n/3) + Θ(n), whose
fractions sum to 1, and the solution is Θ(n log n). Groups of seven work fine but sort more
elements per group for no extra benefit. Five is the smallest size that makes the fractions sum to
less than 1.

**This is where a guaranteed pivot comes from.** Problem 9-3 uses it to give quicksort a Θ(n log n)
worst case — pick the pivot with `SELECT` instead of at random. Nobody ships that either, for the
same reason.

</ComplexityCard>

## The three together

| Question            | Cost               | Guarantee                  |
| ------------------- | ------------------ | -------------------------- |
| Minimum, or maximum | n − 1 comparisons  | Exact, and optimal         |
| Both at once        | 3⌊n/2⌋ comparisons | Exact, and optimal         |
| The i-th smallest   | Θ(n) expected      | Over the algorithm's coins |
| The i-th smallest   | Θ(n) worst case    | Unconditional              |

The pattern to carry forward is the one in §9.2: quicksort and randomized select differ by a single
recursive call, and that one call is the difference between Θ(n log n) and Θ(n). When an algorithm
recurses on every piece it produces, look hard at whether it needs to.
