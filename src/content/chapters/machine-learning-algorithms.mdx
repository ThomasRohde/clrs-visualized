Every algorithm in the book so far has been given a specification and has met it. Sort this. Find
the shortest path. Match the pattern. You could say precisely what the right answer was before the
algorithm ran, which is what made "correct" a meaningful word.

Chapter 33 is not like that. Its three algorithms **search**, and each comes with a guarantee of a
different kind — one weaker than anything in chapter 35, one stronger and stranger, and one that
holds only under an assumption you usually cannot check.

They are also the first algorithms here whose pictures are not structures. There is no array, no
tree, no table: there are points in a space, a running total, the shape of a function. What matters
on screen is a **distance** or a **slope**, which is why this chapter is the one that finally earns
the site its sixth and last renderer.

## §33.1 — Clustering, and an algorithm that promises almost nothing

Split n points into k groups so that each group is tight. Made precise: minimise the sum, over every
point, of the **squared distance to its group's centre**.

Finding the best such split is NP-hard even for k = 2. So this is <a href={chapterHref('approximation-algorithms')}>chapter 35</a>'s predicament
again — except that the standard algorithm comes with no approximation ratio at all.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="k-means" size={21} />

Two steps, alternating. **Assign** every point to the nearest centre. **Update** every centre to the
mean of the points that chose it. Repeat until nothing changes.

**The spokes are the cost.** Each point is drawn joined to the centre it belongs to, and the sum of
the squares of those lengths _is_ the objective function. Watching them shorten is watching the
algorithm work, and when they stop shortening it has finished.

What makes it terminate is that neither step can make things worse:

- assignment moves nothing, so the only change is a point joining a closer centre — which cannot
  raise the total;
- update moves each centre to the mean, and the mean is _by definition_ the point minimising the sum
  of squared distances to a set — so that cannot raise it either.

Finitely many assignments, and the cost never rises: therefore it stops. That is the entire
convergence proof, and notice what it does not say. It says nothing whatever about the answer being
good. The player's `verify` asserts exactly the two things that are true — every point has no nearer
centre, every centre is the mean of its own points — and refuses to assert anything about quality,
because there is nothing there to assert.

In practice this converges in about **three rounds** on the instances here. It is fast, it is
simple, and it will happily hand you a bad clustering with total confidence.

Which is why the seeding matters more than the algorithm. These centres are placed by
**k-means++**: the first at random, then each next one with probability proportional to its squared
distance from the nearest centre already chosen — that is, deliberately far away. Uniform random
seeding routinely drops two centres into one clump and none into another, and **every step
afterwards only goes downhill from where it started**. There is no mechanism for noticing, and none
for recovering. The initialisation is not a detail; on this algorithm it is most of the quality.

## §33.2 — Learning from advice, and a bound that is better than it should be

A room of n advisors. Each round they tell you what to do, you commit, and then the world reveals
who was wrong. You do not know which advisor is any good, and the good one may change.

You cannot beat the best advisor. The question is how close you can come to the best one **in
hindsight** — without ever being told who that is.

<AlgorithmPlayer id="multiplicative-weights" size={18} />

Keep a weight per advisor. Bet in proportion to the weights. After each round, **multiply down the
weight of everyone who was wrong**. That is the whole algorithm, and the guarantee it buys is:

```
L  ≤  (1 + η) · min L_i  +  ln(n)/η
```

Read the second term carefully, because it is the surprising part. The price of not knowing which of
n advisors to trust is **logarithmic in n**. Going from ten advisors to a thousand roughly doubles
it. You can throw an enormous number of hypotheses at this and pay almost nothing for the ones that
turn out to be wrong.

**The picture is the theorem.** Every line is somebody's running total of mistakes; the faint ones
are advisors, the marked one is you. You never dip below the best line — you cannot — but you track
it, having had no way at the start to tell which line that would be. The gap at the right-hand edge
is the **regret**, and over 18 rounds it averages about 3 mistakes here, comfortably inside a bound
that allows about 5.

Nothing is ever set to zero, which is deliberate: an advisor who has a bad week must be able to
recover, because the sequence is not required to be kind and the best advisor in hindsight might be
one who started badly. Multiplying down instead of eliminating is what makes the bound hold against
an adversary rather than merely against noise.

This is the most portable idea in the chapter. The same argument, barely changed, is boosting in
machine learning, the fastest approximate solvers for packing linear programs, and a route to
equilibria in game theory. It is worth recognising when you meet it again.

## §33.3 — Gradient descent, and the assumption underneath everything

Almost every other algorithm in this book computes. This one _looks_ for an answer, by a rule that
fits on one line: **go downhill.**

<AlgorithmPlayer id="gradient-descent" size={8} />

The derivative says which way the ground falls and how steeply. Step against it, in proportion, and
repeat until the ground is flat. The tangent drawn at each step is that gradient; the step taken is
the slope times η.

Two things decide whether this works, and both are knobs on the player.

**The step size η is the slider.** Drag it down and the descent crawls — a dense line of dots
inching along, each step technically correct and going nowhere. Drag it up and each step overshoots
the bottom and lands further up the far side. Past η = 2/f″ the oscillation grows instead of
shrinking and the iterate leaves entirely. There is no safe default, because the threshold depends
on the curvature of the function you happen to be standing on, which you generally do not know.

That conditionality is worth stating precisely, because the player's `verify` had to. Gradient
descent does **not** promise that f falls every step. It promises that f falls when η is small
against the local curvature, and the check computes that condition per step and only then requires
the decrease — a large η is _allowed_ to overshoot, which is exactly what you want to be able to
watch.

**Where you start decides where you end.** This function is deliberately not convex: it has two
valleys of different depths. §33.3's guarantees are all stated for convex functions, where the only
flat point is the global minimum and there is nothing more to say. Here, start to the right of the
ridge and the algorithm settles contentedly into the shallower valley, having done nothing wrong and
with no way of knowing a better one exists.

And it is worse than that, in a way worth seeing once. Type `0.2, 0.3` into the box. That starts the
iterate exactly on the ridge, where the gradient is already zero — so it stops immediately, on a
**maximum**. Flat is the only thing the rule can test for, and the top of a hill is flat.

This loop, on millions of parameters instead of one, is how essentially every modern model is
trained. The guarantees do not get better at that scale; the practice just gets very good at
choosing η.

<ComplexityCard id="gradient-descent">
  **Three algorithms, three grades of promise.** k-means guarantees only that it stops. Gradient
  descent guarantees a decrease, but conditionally on a constant you do not know. Multiplicative
  weights guarantees a bound against an adversary who chose the input after seeing the algorithm —
  by far the strongest of the three, and on the least likely-looking problem.

**Where the answer comes from matters more than usual.** In every earlier chapter the input
determined the output. Here the seeding, the step size and the starting point change the answer, and
none of them is part of the problem statement.

**Local is not global, and nothing here notices the difference.** Both k-means and gradient descent
find fixed points of their own update rule. That is all they look for, and it is all they can check.

</ComplexityCard>

## Where else this shows up

These are the three loops underneath a great deal of what is currently called machine learning.
Gradient descent, with the gradients supplied by backpropagation, trains neural networks; k-means is
the default first thing anyone tries on unlabelled data; multiplicative weights is boosting, and it
is also the algorithm behind a surprising number of results that have nothing to do with learning.

The habit worth taking from this chapter is the one the book has been building since chapter 3.
When an algorithm is described to you as working well, ask what it is guaranteed to do, and then ask
what that guarantee is _conditional on_. Convexity, a step size, a seeding, a bound on the losses.
Every promise in this chapter is real, and every one of them has a precondition that does most of
the work.

Chapter 34's <a href={chapterHref('np-completeness')}>NP-completeness</a> and chapter 35's <a href={chapterHref('approximation-algorithms')}>approximation algorithms</a> are the same
conversation from the other end: there the problem is provably hard and the algorithm is honest
about the factor it gives up. Here the problem is often not even stated as an optimisation you could
be exactly right about, and the algorithm gives up something harder to name.
